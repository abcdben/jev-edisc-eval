Does the model already know this case?

A short guide to the contamination study · 3 October 2026 · companion to the full report and the paper

We are comparing Jev with large language models at deciding which documents are relevant to a legal request. The LLMs were trained on a large slice of the public internet. Our test collections are public. So before trusting a score, we need to know: has the model already seen this material — and does that help it? This guide walks from that question to the answer in eight steps, defining each term where it first appears.

1

The question, made precise

A model can be helped by prior exposure in several different ways. We call any of them contamination: the test material, or knowledge that makes the test easier, was in the model’s training data (the text it learned from, which ends at a cutoff date). We split the question in two.

  • Does the model know the documents? The e-mails themselves, the people who wrote them, the benchmark’s questions, the published answers.
  • Does the model know the case? Who the parties were, what was alleged, who did what, how it ended — the kind of knowledge a lawyer who followed the news would have, with or without ever seeing an e-mail.

The second question is the one specific to legal review, and it is the one a normal contamination test misses.

2

The setup: five public corpora, one made up

Four of the study’s six collections are real and old enough to be in training data: the Enron e-mails (public since 2003), the Jeb Bush e-mails (2015), the Mallinckrodt opioid-litigation e-mails (2021–23) and the CUAD contracts (an NLP benchmark from 2021). The fifth, Endo, is real but late: an opioid-litigation production published in 2024–26, after the models stopped learning, from a case that was in the news for years. The sixth, Veridian, is a fictional hip-implant lawsuit we wrote in September 2026, with invented company, people and documents. No model can know it. It is our floor: the score you get when you know nothing.

We also need a ceiling — material every model has certainly seen many times — so we know what "contaminated" looks like on each scale: the Federalist Papers and Constitution, the Kaggle Titanic passenger list (a famous spreadsheet copied onto millions of websites), and for the case-level tests, U.S. v. Microsoft.

Every test is run the same way on every collection, on three OpenAI models of increasing size (GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Sol), and every number is read as a position between the floor and the ceiling. The whole battery cost about $18.

3

Testing for the documents (Part I)

Four quick tests, one per way the documents could be known:

Verbatim
Show the model the first 150 words of a document; ask for the next 60. Score how much it reproduces exactly. LCS-F1 is the overlap score we use (1.0 = word-perfect, ~0.15 = a plausible guess in the right style); we also count the longest run of consecutive words it gets right.
People
Take 30 names from each mailbox — famous ones, middling ones and obscure ones. Ask "who is this?" (recall) and "was this person at Enron?" (recognition), the latter also for four wrong companies. False alarms — saying yes to the wrong company — tell us whether a "yes" means anything. d′ ("d-prime") combines hits and false alarms into one number: 0 means the model cannot tell the right company from the wrong one; about 4.8 is perfect here.
Benchmark
Ask the model to list the benchmark’s own questions and categories from memory.
Labels
Give only a document’s ID number and ask whether it was judged relevant. We balance relevant and not-relevant within each topic so that guessing from the topic scores exactly 50%: anything above that is memorised answers.

Two statistics appear throughout. A 95% confidence interval is the range of values consistent with the data; if it excludes a number (say 50%), the result is unlikely to be luck. A p-value is the probability of seeing a difference this large if there were really none; below 0.05 we call it significant.

4

What the floor and ceiling look like

The controls behave, which is what lets us read everything else. On the verbatim test, Veridian scores LCS-F1 0.15 / 0.15 / 0.17 across the three models — the floor is not zero, because a good guess in the right style earns something. The largest model reproduces the Federalist Papers at 0.94 and copies Titanic rows, ticket numbers and all, in 100% of chunks. It recognises every one of the framers of the Constitution with zero false alarms (d′ 4.9). And on the label test it guesses Titanic passengers’ survival from their row and name 84% of the time against a 50% ceiling. Bigger models memorise more: on every anchor the order is Luna < Terra < Sol.

5

The real collections, at the document level

The e-mail collections sit at the floor; we found no evidence of memorised e-mail. Enron 0.16 / 0.16 / 0.17, Jeb Bush 0.15 / 0.16 / 0.15, Mallinckrodt 0.16 / 0.15 / 0.16, Endo 0.16 / 0.14 / 0.15 — within a few hundredths of Veridian, on the e-mails the models agreed to continue. We read every long run by hand: they were a repeated error message, a list of internet jokes, a government notice quoted inside an e-mail, and they are screened out. No e-mail body was reproduced in 60 windows per collection — which is absence of evidence, made informative by the same test finding the Federalist Papers and the contracts. (The Enron e-mails are in public training sets, so the models have very likely seen them; what we do not find is memory of them, which in the literature comes from repetition, not presence.) The models know the famous executives (d′ 2.3 on Enron for the largest model) and almost nobody else: the obscure-name tier is at 0%. Nobody can list the TREC benchmark’s questions. Label guessing is at chance everywhere (51% / 50% / 49% on Enron).

CUAD is the exception. Its contracts are SEC filings and the benchmark is well known: LCS-F1 0.25 / 0.27 / 0.29, with the largest model reproducing 15 or more consecutive words in 10% of excerpts, and all 41 clause categories recited from memory. That is consistent with memorisation of republished public contracts; one caveat is that our floor is e-mail, and legal drafting is more predictable than e-mail, so part of the gap may be the genre. A set of contracts filed after the models’ cutoff would settle it.

6

Testing for the case (Part II)

Now the legal question. Four tests, each matching something a lawyer who knows the case would have:

M0 · Recognise it
Show a description of the matter with every name removed or changed — including the TREC benchmark’s own pseudonymised complaints, which rename Enron "Volteron" — and ask which real case it is.
M1 · Recall the record
"Describe the Enron scandal." Grade against a checklist of 16–20 facts: parties, allegations, people, events, outcome.
M2 · Predict the evidence
Give the model only what it gets in the study — the matter and one request — and ask what it expects responsive documents to contain. Then check each named thing against the actual labelled documents. A term is discriminative if it really appears in responsive documents at least twice as often as in documents overall (lift ≥ 2). This is the test of whether the knowledge is usable.
M3 · Read the headers
Judge relevance from an e-mail’s headers only, twice: with and without From/To/Cc. The difference is what knowing the people is worth.

Veridian is again the floor (the right answers are "no real case" and "I don’t know this matter"); U.S. v. Microsoft is the ceiling. Enron gives us two clean comparisons: its scandal requests against unrelated oil-spill requests on the same mailbox, and the pseudonym against the real name.

7

What the case tests found

The pseudonyms don’t hold. Every model identifies the Deepwater Horizon complaint; "Volteron" is decoded as Enron by two of three (the third says Reliant Energy — a real company in the same scandal). Two models even declare the fictional Veridian to be Zimmer Biomet, because the sketch says Warsaw, Indiana.

Enron is known like a textbook case. 85% / 80% / 90% of the checklist — the same as Microsoft (88% / 94% / 88%). Mallinckrodt is known as a settlement and a bankruptcy, not as a set of allegations (48% / 43% / 52%); Endo, whose e-mails were published after the cutoff, is known a little better (70% / 55% / 70%) — the case, not the documents. Jeb Bush’s controversies are known; the people in his mailbox are not (50% / 44% / 67%, people 0%). All three models say they do not know Veridian.

The knowledge is usable — once the case is named. Under TREC’s pseudonym the models offer 1 / 2 / 0 discriminative real names for the Enron requests. Name the company and the same requests yield 12 / 24 / 46: the deal names, the special-purpose vehicles, the Andersen partners who did the shredding, each 3–12 times more common in responsive documents. The unrelated oil-spill requests on the same mailbox yield 3 / 0 / 1; Veridian yields 0 / 2 / 3.

But knowing the people adds almost nothing to a header-level call. From the subject line alone the models are already at 80% / 82% / 83% on Enron; adding who sent and received the e-mail changes that by +1% / -1% / +2%. The knowledge is about what the documents say, which a header does not show.

8

Putting the study's cases on a ladder of real ones

A floor and a ceiling tell you the ends of the scale, not where "medium" is. So we ran the recognition and recall tests on 24 more real matters from the last ninety years — frauds like WorldCom and a 1938 drug-wholesaler scandal, opioid cases like Purdue, e-mail scandals like the Clinton server, device cases like the Dalkon Shield — plus two SEC cases filed in 2026, after the models’ training ended, as a real-but-unseen floor. For each we also recorded how much public attention it gets (Wikipedia pageviews).

Three things stand out. Nothing that was ever litigated in public comes out blank: even matters with no Wikipedia article are recalled at 80–100% by the larger models. Fame predicts knowledge for the smaller models (rank correlation Luna +0.48 (p = 0.015), Terra +0.48 (p = 0.014)) but not measurably for the largest (+0.25, p = 0.22) — at that scale, "obscure" looks like no protection; only "after the cutoff" is. The nameless sketches identified 26 of 27 real matters for every model; the one miss is Sol on Enron (it names Reliant Energy, a company in the same scandal). And the 2026 cases behave like Veridian: the models say they do not know them (one of them, asked to identify the matter, confidently names a case that does not exist). Among real cases, Enron sits at the very top with Bhopal and the Clinton server; Mallinckrodt is the least-known real matter on the whole ladder, with Endo just above it; Jeb Bush is in between. Endo is the study’s clearest case: the models know the matter, and its documents were published after their training ended — the date is the proof, and the probes agree, showing no document-level signal — so it is the collection we recommend testing on from now on.

9

So does the knowledge actually change the calls?

Analogy. Change the names on every exhibit and run the review again. A reviewer who was trading on who the people were should do worse the second time; one who was reading the documents should do the same.

Think of it as changing the names on every exhibit and running the review again. We took the Enron e-mails, consistently replaced every person and company with an invented one — the same fake name every time a real one appeared — and asked each system to make the same relevance calls on the renamed set. If a system had been leaning on what it already knew about Enron’s people and deals, its score should fall when the names it recognises are taken away. For comparison we did the same on the unrelated oil-spill requests from the same mailbox (where there is little to know) and on the fictional Veridian collection (where renaming can only cost).

The scores barely moved. On the Enron scandal requests the change in F1 was -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev); on the control requests -1.8 / +0.6 / -1.0 / -1.7; on Veridian -0.1 / -0.4 / -1.0 / -0.3. The difference that measures the knowledge — scandal requests minus control requests — was GPT-5.6 Luna +1.6 [-1.2, +4.6]; GPT-5.6 Terra -2.6 [-5.8, +0.5]; GPT-5.6 Sol +2.1 [-0.6, +4.6]; Jev +0.0 [-2.6, +2.6], and no system’s interval excludes zero. Renaming changed about 2–5% of individual calls either way, which is the noise the operation itself introduces.

One caveat in plain words. When we afterwards asked the models which company the renamed documents came from, they still said Enron for 61% / 82% / 94% of them (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol) — they recognised the case from the story itself, not from the names. So renaming is not a clean test of what the knowledge is worth: the small effect we measured is a floor, not a ceiling. It does show that the names are not how the knowledge would help.

Jev. The classifier under evaluation went through the same test. Its vendor says it was not trained on public text; we treat that as a claim. Overall its result was the same as the language models’ (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6] points), which is consistent with the claim. On one request its score did fall when the names were changed (-18.2 points on FAS 140, and the documents it lost are largely the ones naming Enron’s off-books vehicles). That is the one place in the renaming test where the evidence points the other way. We followed it up with tests built for a classifier: a bare vehicle name on its own does not trigger the request (0% responsive as written, 78% once the request names the vehicles), and a code-name swap on four matters shows Jev, like the language models, recognises the real names of the cases. In plain terms: the tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections, and they show it knows the cases behind them about as well as the language models do; knowing the case did not measurably change its Enron score.

Then we did it two more ways. On the contracts the models can partly recite, we rewrote every excerpt in different words (same parties, same numbers, same obligations) so the memorised wording was gone; on the Florida e-mails we renamed the Governor and the public figures and compared the topics about his governorship with topics on the same documents that are not; and on the fictional matter we did the opposite and handed the reviewer the case file.

Two more collections, two more ways to take the knowledge away. On the public contracts — the one collection the models have partly memorised word for word — we changed the parties, dates and amounts, and separately rewrote every excerpt in different words while keeping every party, term, number and obligation (a second model checked 89 of 100 rewrites as legally equivalent). Renaming moved the scores by -0.6 / -0.7 / +0.2 / +0.0 points and rewriting by -1.3 / -0.3 / -0.4 / -0.3 (Luna / Terra / Sol / Jev) — about what the same rewrite costs on the invented collection, and no more on the contracts the models remember best. On the Jeb Bush e-mails we renamed the Governor, his family and Florida's public figures and compared the requests about famous events (the recount, Terri Schiavo) with requests about ordinary state business on the same e-mails: two of the three language models' scores did not move and Terra's slipped by about 2 points (Luna +1.0; Terra -2.3; Sol +0.1 net), even though GPT-5.6 Luna could still tell whose e-mails they were 69% of the time. Jev's moved more: -3.1 points net on the famous-event requests, with its recall on the recount and George W. Bush requests falling when the names went. That looks like a classifier that recognises public figures and leans on them a little — the kind of knowledge any system trained on public text has — not like one that has seen these e-mails or their answer key.

Four more ways the result could have failed, and did not. We tagged every Enron document for whether its relevance could only be seen with outside knowledge of the case: only about 7 in 100 are like that, and on those documents every system did worse, not better (61% / 61% / 64% / 66% vs 83% / 81% / 83% / 78% accuracy), which is the opposite of what cheating would look like. We handed every system a one-page briefing on the invented Veridian matter — the one case none of them could know — and the scores moved by at most 1.0 points, well within chance. We wrote short documents that contradict a famous fact (a well-known opioid described as an antacid) and found the systems do sometimes trust the fact over the page (8–17% of the time), but no more often than when the fact came from the briefing in front of them. And we checked whether the systems rank the same way on every collection: they do not, and the gap between the language models and Jev is largest on the best-known matter — a pattern that could be knowledge or could be the kind of collection, and which we could not settle with four systems.

The conclusion, in four sentences

  1. At the level of documents and people, we found no evidence that the e-mail collections are inside the models — they score within a hair of a fictional corpus on a test that does catch memorised text — while CUAD scores above that floor, consistent with memorised public contracts.
  2. At the level of the case, Enron is thoroughly inside the models, recognisable through a pseudonym and usable as search terms once named; Mallinckrodt and Jeb Bush much less so. Endo — a known case whose e-mails were published after the cutoff — shows the same split from the other side.
  3. For legal review, then, the evidence points to the matter, not the document, as the unit of contamination; a test aimed at documents would have cleared Enron, and renaming the complaint is no defence.
  4. Whether that knowledge raises a model’s review score was then measured directly, on three matters and in three ways: renaming every person and organisation in the Enron e-mails, renaming the public figures in the Florida e-mails, rewording the contracts the models can recite, and handing over the case file for the invented matter. For the three LLMs none of it changed the review score by more than 2.6 points once the control is subtracted, and every interval includes zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]). That is a bound, not a proof of no effect — the models still recognised Enron from the story in 61% / 82% / 94% of the renamed documents — so the clean test remains a matter the models have not seen. Jev did show one small dependence on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics): the kind of familiarity a system with ordinary public knowledge of famous people would show, worth a few points on a famous-figure collection, and not by itself evidence that it was trained on the collection.

Glossary

Training data / cutoff
The text a model learned from, and the date after which nothing is included.
Contamination
Test material, or knowledge that makes the test easier, being in the training data. Exposure = it is in there; effect = the score benefits.
Floor / ceiling
What a model scores knowing nothing (a fictional corpus) and knowing everything (famous texts), measured with the same test.
LCS-F1
Overlap between the model's continuation and the true text, based on the longest common subsequence of words; 1.0 is word-perfect.
Hit / false alarm / d′
Saying "yes" when the answer is yes / when it is no; d′ is the gap between the two expressed in standard-deviation units (0 = no discrimination).
Confidence interval
The range of values consistent with the data at a stated level (here 95%). Wilson intervals are used for proportions, bootstrap intervals for means and paired differences.
p-value
The probability of a result at least this extreme if there were truly no effect; below 0.05 is called significant.
Bootstrap
Re-sampling the data with replacement many times to see how much a statistic would vary.
Spearman rank correlation (ρ)
How well the ordering of one quantity predicts the ordering of another, from −1 to +1.
Pseudonymised
Real names replaced with invented ones; the facts are left intact.
Lift
How many times more often a term appears in responsive documents than in documents overall; ≥ 2 is our threshold for "discriminative".
MDL
Multidistrict litigation: many related federal lawsuits consolidated before one judge for pre-trial proceedings.
Bellwether
An early test trial in a mass tort, used to gauge how juries value the claims.
TREC
The Text REtrieval Conference, whose Legal and Total Recall tracks produced the Enron and Jeb Bush test collections and their relevance judgments.
