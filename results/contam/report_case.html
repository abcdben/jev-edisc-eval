Part II — Is the case inside the model?

Methodology

13. Why the case matters: the legal frame

Part I treated the corpora as text: are the documents memorised, are the correspondents known, are the benchmark's topics and labels known? It found the e-mail corpora near the floor on documents and the cast known only where it is famous. That is reassuring, and it is also the wrong unit of analysis for legal discovery.

A relevance review is not defined by a corpus. It is defined by a matter: a complaint, the allegations in it, the parties and their people, the factual record the parties will fight over, and — once it is over — the outcome. A reviewer who already knows the case reads every document differently from one who does not. They know which transactions were later found to be fraudulent, which executives were indicted, which code names mattered, which customers were the "pill mills", which year the shredding happened. They can tell that an e-mail from the CFO about an offshore vehicle is responsive to a request about debt-concealment without the request saying "offshore vehicle". The request text and the task context we give every model are the same; the prior each model brings to them is not.

Four of the study's six corpora come from matters that are among the most-documented in American legal history. Enron is the textbook corporate fraud; the Mallinckrodt and Endo documents come from the opioid MDL, the largest civil litigation of its era; the Jeb Bush e-mails are the public record of a two-term governorship whose controversies (the 2000 recount, Terri Schiavo) were national news. Jev has read none of this. If a frontier model has, then on these corpora it is not a fresh reviewer but a reviewer who has read the press, the indictments and the settlements — and its score is partly a score for hindsight.

So Part II asks a different question from Part I: not "has the model seen these documents?" but "does the model know this case, and is what it knows the kind of knowledge that changes a relevance call?" Four things a case-aware reviewer has, each of which becomes a probe:

M2 is the bridge: it measures whether the exposure is actionable, by checking the model's expectations against the documents' actual labels. 14. The matter probes and their controls

M0 · Matter identification

For Enron we use the TREC Legal Track's own pleadings, which were written to be fictional: Complaint J puts Enron's exact class period (June 1, 1999 – December 2, 2001), prepay transactions, FAS 140, shredding and energy-schedule manipulation under the name "Volteron Corp."; Complaint K is an oil-spill class action over the sinking of the "Bleak Horizon". The model sees ~2,500 characters of each complaint and is asked which real company or case it is modelled on. For Mallinckrodt, Endo, Jeb Bush, Veridian and the ceiling matter we write a short sketch with every proper noun removed. A hit names the real matter. For Veridian the correct answer is "no real matter"; we record whether the model instead names a real template (DePuy ASR, Zimmer Biomet, Stryker…), because a synthetic matter built on a famous archetype inherits some of that archetype's knowledge.

M1 · Matter recall against a checklist

"Describe {matter}: parties, key allegations, key people, main events with dates, outcome." Graded against a hand-written checklist of 16–20 facts per matter spanning five categories (parties, allegations, people, events, outcome), keyword-matched on whole words. Two refinements make the number meaningful for the study. First, every item is flagged as in context or beyond context: the study's task context already tells every model that Mallinckrodt is accused of weak suspicious-order monitoring, so reciting that is not an advantage; knowing about the 2017 DEA settlement or the Chapter 11 is. We report both the total and the beyond-context share. Second, the Veridian matter is asked under its full fictional caption (MDL No. 3102, N.D. Ind.); the right answer is "I do not know this matter", and anything else is a confabulation whose length we record.

M2 · Evidence prior

The model is given exactly what it gets in the study — the matter context and one request — and asked, as the review lead with no documents yet seen, for the 25 most specific things it expects in responsive documents, each typed as a person, organisation, code name, product, place, period or keyword. Scoring is against the labelled corpus. Terms already present in the context or request are discarded (the model was told those). Of the remaining named terms we ask two questions: is it grounded — does it actually occur in at least two judged documents? — and is it discriminative — is it at least twice as frequent among responsive documents as overall (lift ≥ 2, in ≥ 2 responsive documents)? A grounded, discriminative named term is a piece of case knowledge that a search or a classifier could use directly. Generic keywords ("spreadsheet", "wholesaler") are scored the same way but reported separately: they measure vocabulary, not knowledge of the case.

Enron supplies two built-in contrasts. The seven Complaint J requests (201–207) are the real scandal — prepay, FAS 140, forecasts, shredding, energy schedules, analysts — plus one control topic, fantasy football, that TREC added for exactly this reason; the three Complaint K requests (301–303: oil and gas drilling, spill response, lobbying) are not Enron's story at all. And the Complaint J requests are run twice: once as TREC wrote them, under the "Volteron" pseudonym, and once with the company named. The difference between those two runs is the knowledge that the pseudonym suppressed, and the difference between J and K is the knowledge that is specific to the actual case.

M3 · Metadata-only relevance

For each request we draw a balanced sample of responsive and non-responsive e-mails (20 + 20; 15 + 15 for Jeb Bush) and show the model the matter context, the request and only the header metadata — no body. Each e-mail is shown twice: with Date, From, To, Cc and Subject, and with Date and Subject alone. The paired difference in accuracy is what seeing the people added. Because the sample is balanced within every request, chance is 50% and the request itself carries no label information; a lexical baseline (a request-title word appearing in the subject line) shows what plain keyword matching achieves on the same e-mails. Veridian is the control for the people channel: its names are fictional, so any lift its headers give comes from structure (domains, distribution lists, FDA addresses), not from knowing who anyone is. For Enron the context states that "Volteron" is Enron, so that the headers condition does not also reveal which company it is.

The ladder · real matters at graded exposure

Two anchors fix the ends of a scale; they do not show its shape. To read the study's matters as positions rather than as isolated numbers, M0 and M1 are also run on a ladder of 24 real matters from the last ninety years, chosen in families that mirror the corpora: accounting and securities frauds for Enron (WorldCom, HealthSouth, Peregrine Systems, Equity Funding, McKesson & Robbins); opioid cases for Mallinckrodt and Endo (Purdue, Insys, Rochester Drug Co-operative, Teva, JUUL as the adjacent public-nuisance case); e-mail in public life for Jeb Bush (the Clinton server, Bridgegate, the Sony Pictures hack); device mass torts for Veridian (Dalkon Shield, DePuy ASR, the 3M earplugs, Bair Hugger); landmark disputes across the century (Bhopal, Texaco v. Pennzoil, Dieselgate, Theranos, FTX); and two post-cutoff controls — SEC accounting-fraud actions filed in 2026 against the former officers of Near Intelligence and against Meyer Global Management — which are real but cannot be in the training data. Each rung gets the same de-identified sketch and the same checklist grading as the study's matters (10–18 facts each). Alongside, an independent proxy for public exposure is recorded for every matter: the length, number of language editions and last-twelve-months pageviews of its English Wikipedia article (none for the fictional and post-cutoff matters, and for a few real ones that have no article). The expectation is a gradient — recall rising with footprint within each family — with the post-cutoff matters behaving like Veridian.

The fictional matter shows the other failure. GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra state confidently that Veridian is Zimmer Biomet — because the sketch says Warsaw, Indiana and metal-on-metal, and Warsaw is where Zimmer Biomet is. So the synthetic control is clean at the level of documents and people, but its archetype is recognised, and a model reviewing Veridian documents is bringing some knowledge of how metal-on-metal hip litigation went. That is the floor's one impurity, and it is now measured rather than assumed.

The anchors fixed the ends of the scale; the ladder shows its shape, on matters chosen to resemble the study's own. Three things stand out. First, the floor for a real, litigated matter is high. The lowest real rung for each model is GPT-5.6 Luna: Mallinckrodt opioid litigation at 39%, GPT-5.6 Terra: Peregrine Systems at 33%, GPT-5.6 Sol: Mallinckrodt opioid litigation at 44%; nothing that was ever charged, tried or settled in public comes out blank. A distributor criminally charged in 2019 that has no Wikipedia article at all, and the largest MDL in history, which also has none, are recalled at Rochester Drug Co-operative 40% / 80% / 90%; 3M Combat Arms earplugs 75% / 100% / 100%. Legal matters live in sources — DOJ and SEC press releases, dockets, opinions, law-firm client alerts — that are crawled thoroughly and read rarely, so public popularity understates what the models carry.

Second, scale flattens the ladder. Across the matters with an article, the rank correlation between twelve-month pageviews and recall is GPT-5.6 Luna +0.48 (p = 0.015), GPT-5.6 Terra +0.48 (p = 0.014), GPT-5.6 Sol +0.25 (not significant, p = 0.22). For the small and mid models, fame predicts knowledge: a 1938 wholesaler fraud or a 1973 insurance fraud is half-known, a 2002 fraud that made the business pages for a season is two-thirds known, a byword is fully known. For GPT-5.6 Sol the correlation is not significant at 27 rungs — Texaco v. Pennzoil, HealthSouth, Dalkon Shield and Rochester Drug all score ≥ 90% — consistent with it knowing the obscure rungs about as well as the famous ones, though 27 matters is a small sample for the comparison. The practical consequence for the study is that "obscure" is not a defence at frontier scale; only "after the cutoff" is.

Third, identification is nearly binary. 26 of the 27 real matters are named by every model from a sketch with every proper noun removed; Enron is missed only by GPT-5.6 Sol. Recognition of a fact pattern precedes recall of its details and is almost complete for anything that reached the national press, however long ago. A pseudonymised complaint or a redacted request is identifying information for any real matter the model has met at all; §15 showed that for Enron, and the ladder shows it is the rule.

The post-cutoff rungs are the control the fictional matter cannot supply: real cases the models cannot have read about. For the September 2026 adviser case, GPT-5.6 Luna said it could not tell, GPT-5.6 Terra named a different case, GPT-5.6 Sol said it could not tell; asked to describe the case by name, all three decline (0% / 0% / 0% of the facts the question did not state). GPT-5.6 Terra's answer is worth reading: it names a specific prior SEC case, defendant and filing date that we cannot find and whose particulars reproduce the sketch — a confident, concrete identification of a matter that does not exist in its training data, where the other two models say the date is beyond what they can verify. Near Intelligence straddles the cutoff and behaves accordingly: GPT-5.6 Luna named it, GPT-5.6 Terra named it, GPT-5.6 Sol named it; recall beyond the question 22% / 0% / 0%. The models know the company's December 2023 collapse (it was news) and decline to describe the 2026 complaint; GPT-5.6 Sol dates itself to a June 2024 cutoff in its answer. One caveat: the sketches carry their dates, and the models use "2026 is in the future" as a reason to decline. A stricter version would omit the date and see whether they still know they do not know.

Where the study's matters sit. Enron (84% / 79% / 90%) is in the top group with Bhopal, the Clinton server, Microsoft and WorldCom — the matters the models know almost entirely. Jeb Bush (50% / 44% / 67%) is well below the two e-mail scandals that share its family (Bridgegate 92% / 92% / 100%, the Clinton server 93% / 87% / 100%): a governorship is remembered as a list of controversies, not as a cast. Mallinckrodt (39% / 33% / 44%) is the least-known real matter on the whole ladder for every model — below Rochester Drug and Insys, its opioid-family peers, and below a 1938 fraud. That is the useful surprise: of the real e-mail corpora, the one whose matter the models know least is a recent one, because its record is an MDL docket and a bankruptcy rather than a trial with a narrative. Endo (62% / 44% / 62%) sits just above it: the matter is known at least as well as Mallinckrodt's by every model despite a smaller public footprint (13,340 pageviews against 36,785), while its documents, published after the cutoff, are at the floor — the ladder shows the case and Part I shows the collection, and they disagree in the direction the study wants. Its opioid peers added as rungs confirm the family: Teva (83% / 92% / 83%) and JUUL (67% / 60% / 73%), with footprints of 115,631 and 98,572, are identified by every model and recalled at the Enron end of the scale. Veridian sits at the bottom with the post-cutoff case, where a floor should be. The ladder turns Part II's "high / medium / low" into positions among named peers, and it gives the study a way to place any future corpus before spending money on it: find the matter's rung, or — for a small model — read it off the footprint.

18. M2 · Is the knowledge actionable? The evidence prior against the labels

This is the probe that turns exposure into something a classifier could use, and it produces the sharpest result in the study. Under TREC's pseudonym the models volunteer almost no real names for the Enron scandal requests — 0.4 / 1.1 / 0.4 named terms per request beyond the prompt, 1 / 2 / 0 discriminative in total across seven requests — and their notes say why: "I know only the supplied fictionalized complaint summary." Name the company and the same requests yield 5.9 / 10.3 / 14.7 named terms per request, 88% / 86% / 81% of them real, and 12 / 24 / 46 discriminative named terms (Luna / Terra / Sol). For GPT-5.6 Sol they are the actual record of the case: Mahonia and Yosemite and Delta for prepay; Raptor I–IV, Talon and Chewco for FAS 140; David Duncan, Nancy Temple and Michael Odom — the Andersen partners in the shredding — for document destruction; Fastow, Skilling, Lay and the analysts' banks for analyst contacts. Each is over-represented among the responsive documents by a factor of 2.7 to 11.7. A reviewer who searched for them first would be most of the way to the responsive set before reading anything.

The Complaint K requests are the within-corpus control: same mailbox, same models, requests that are not Enron's story. They yield 3 / 0 / 1 discriminative named terms. The knowledge is specific to the case, not to the corpus. Mallinckrodt sits low (2 / 1 / 4) but not at zero: GPT-5.6 Sol names Xartemis XR, a Mallinckrodt opioid the task context does not mention, Broward County and South Florida for the pill-mill request, and DEA's Office of Diversion Control for quota — the vocabulary of someone who has read the complaints. Jeb Bush yields 16 / 18 / 19 on a 600-document subset, with real Florida officials at the top (Coleman Stipanovich at the State Board of Administration for Movie Gallery, Jerry Regier at DCF for Rilya Wilson, Alan Levine at AHCA for Medicaid), which is the "surrounding details" channel Part I only glimpsed through the recognition matrix.

Veridian gives the floor its number: 0 / 2 / 3 discriminative named terms across ten requests, and they are domain institutions rather than case facts — FDA, the NJR and AJRR joint registries. Generic keywords (right-hand chart) discriminate at 20–40% on every corpus including Veridian; that is vocabulary, and it is what a reviewer with no knowledge of the case brings. The named-term rate is the case-knowledge signal, and it separates the pseudonymised Enron, the named Enron and the fictional matter by an order of magnitude.

Generalisation checks: four ways the result could have failed

Four checks on the claim on the matters tested, knowing the case changed review accuracy by at most a few F1 points, each chosen so that it could have undermined it; each is read in claim language and Jev is a system under test throughout. Full tables and figures in the verification report. New OpenAI spend $10.95 of a $15 cap.

  • A · Knowledge-dependence (Enron J). Only 6.9% [5.4, 8.9] of the Enron J documents are knowledge-dependent (relevance visible only with outside Enron knowledge; FAS 140 25%, every other request ≤ 8%). Contamination predicts higher named accuracy on them and a larger renaming drop on them. Neither holds: every system is less accurate on them when named (61% / 61% / 64% / 66% vs 83% / 81% / 83% / 78%; Luna / Terra / Sol / Jev), and the most accurate system on them is Jev. Renaming on the knowledge-dependent subset (n = 59) moved F1 by Luna +4.1 [-6.7, +14.9]; Terra +4.0 [-17.6, +26.2]; Sol +17.3 [+4.8, +32.1]; Jev -15.7 [-33.3, -0.7] — renaming helped GPT-5.6 Sol; the one drop whose interval excludes zero is Jev's. The LLMs' Enron J edge over Jev sits on the self-contained documents. Strengthens the claim: where knowledge could help, no system behaves as if it did.
  • B · Ranking stability. System order is not stable across corpora: Kendall W = 0.14 over 5 corpora × 4 systems (p ≈ 0.56; four objects give W almost no power). Enron J: Sol > Luna > Terra > Jev; Enron K: Sol > Luna > Jev > Terra; Mallinckrodt: Terra > Jev > Sol > Luna; Veridian: Jev > Terra > Sol > Luna; Endo: Terra > Sol > Luna > Jev. Within each LLM, F1 does not trend with its own case-knowledge score (Spearman ρ Luna +0.0, Terra -0.2, Sol -0.2, n = 4). The mean-LLM − Jev F1 gap grows monotonically with the LLMs' case knowledge: Enron J (case 84) +6.3 → Mallinckrodt (case 53) -2.0 → Veridian (case 0) -4.1 pp. That is the pattern contamination predicts, but it is also what corpus type predicts (real human-judged e-mail at 49% prevalence on Enron J against LLM-panel and synthetic-planner gold at 11% and 10% on Mallinckrodt and Veridian), and checks A and D test the mechanism directly on both ends of the gradient and find none. Ambiguous, leaning against the claim on its own; unresolved at n = 4.
  • C · Counterfactual conflicts. On 30 short documents per matter whose text contradicts a well-known fact (Roxicodone as an antacid; the Schiavo file as a road renaming), the call follows world knowledge rather than the text on 8–17% of pairs pooled over the four real matters (Luna 17% / Terra 14% / Sol 12% / Jev 8%; every system reads the factual versions at ≥ 99%), almost entirely in one direction — a well-known responsive token keeps a document responsive — and concentrated on Jeb Bush, Mallinckrodt and Endo, near zero on Enron. Mixed; neutral for the claim as stated: the systems do let a prior fact outweigh the page on an engineered conflict, Jev least, but no more for pre-trained facts than for in-context ones, and check A says such conflicts are rare on real documents.

A 5,726-character fictional case brief — parties, products, people, deals, code names, timeline, outcome — appended to the Veridian context exactly as the Mallinckrodt brief was, on 491 of the 1000 ablation documents. 21. Classifier-native tests: Jev through its own interface

Every probe above except M3 is a generative question, and the ablation (§20) removed one channel — proper names in full documents. These tests use only what a classifier offers: a relevance call and its probability. Each is a paired design with bootstrap intervals; the GPT-5.6 models (GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Sol) are the positive comparison (systems known to carry matter knowledge) and Veridian the synthetic floor, so no system here is a clean reference. Jev is a system under test throughout, and every result is stated as evidence consistent or inconsistent with its vendor's statement that it is trained on synthetic data — never as proof. Method, per-token tables and the full T4 breakdown are in the classifier-native report. Spend: OpenAI $5.14, Jev $0.15.

23. Caveats

  • Three models, one vendor. The Anthropic and Gemini arms of the study have not been probed; the runner will fill them in when keys are available. The within-vendor scale gradient suggests the pattern will hold, but that is an expectation, not a measurement.
  • Embedded public text (government notices, licence banners, jokes) is memorised from its public source and the verbatim probe cannot tell that apart from corpus memorisation. The top runs per corpus were inspected by hand; a cross-check against a web index would automate it.
  • CUAD's floor is not genre-matched. Veridian is e-mail; legal drafting is more predictable than e-mail, so part of CUAD's gap above the floor may be genre rather than memorisation even after standard clauses and mirrored provisions are filtered out of the pool. The long party-specific runs are the number that cannot be explained that way. A post-cutoff EDGAR contract sample is the control that would separate the two, and is the next addition to the battery.
  • The document-level null is absence of evidence, from 60 windows per corpus, 60 words each, at temperature 0, with one prompt form. It is informative because the same probe fires on the canon anchor and on CUAD, and it is bounded: a model could hold a few documents of a 46,000-document mailbox verbatim without a 60-window sample finding them. The Enron corpus is in open training sets (The Pile), so presence in training data is likely; what is not detectable is document-level memory, which in the literature tracks repetition rather than presence.
  • Refusals. GPT-5.6 Luna declines to continue 25% of Veridian's and 15% of Endo's e-mails as private, and 0% of Enron's; the other models 0%. Refusals are excluded from every V statistic on both sides; scored as zeros they had masked the floor for Luna, and the earlier reading of a small-model residual above the floor as a genre effect is withdrawn.
  • The pool filter is a judgement. The rules are deterministic; the screen is a small model at temperature 0 whose verdicts are cached and inspectable (results/contam/v_screen.jsonl). It is lenient rather than strict — a few predictable continuations survive it — and the novel flag and the per-item review page are the backstop.
  • Jeb Bush text is available locally only for the 600-document subset, so its name tiers are shallow (the top tier starts at 14 documents) and its verbatim sample is drawn from a narrower pool than the others.
  • Name collisions. A common name may belong to a better-known person (a Veridian tail name collided with a real analyst). Grading on the target organisation makes collisions a miss, which is conservative.
  • Benchmark grading is keyword-based and coarse; one of Sol's seven TREC 2009 guesses ("204 — document destruction") matched by what is probably coincidence.
  • Exposure is mostly not effect. Parts I and the first three matter probes measure what is in the model; M3 and the ablation (§20) measure a change in a relevance call. The round-1 ablation removes only the names, and the models still recognise the case from the fact pattern in most renamed documents, so its small Δ is a lower bound on the effect of case knowledge, not the whole of it; round 2 adds paraphrase (which removes the memorised text) and the brief-injection check adds the knowledge instead of removing it, and neither moved the LLMs' F1 beyond noise.
  • Jev's familiarity effect is small and is not a contamination verdict. Its matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics; FAS 140 on Enron; the knowledge-dependent Enron documents), which is what an encoder with public-web knowledge of public figures would show; it is not by itself evidence of training on the collections or their labels, and T2–T4 and the CUAD paraphrase find no sign of either. For evaluations: a Jev score on a famous-figure collection may run a few points high relative to an unknown matter.
  • The CUAD paraphrase control is not genre-matched. The cost of paraphrase was measured on Veridian e-mail, not on unseen contracts; a post-cutoff EDGAR sample with expert clause labels is the clean control and was not affordable. The within-CUAD dose–response (no growth of Δ with the contract's memorisation score) is the primary control.
  • System ordering is not stable across corpora, and the LLM − Jev gap widens with the LLMs' case knowledge. Kendall W = 0.14 over the four systems on 5 corpora; the mean-LLM − Jev F1 gap runs Enron J (case 84) +6.3 → Mallinckrodt (case 53) -2.0 → Veridian (case 0) -4.1 pp. Contamination predicts this ordering, but so does corpus type (real human-judged e-mail at 49% prevalence on Enron J against LLM-panel and synthetic-planner gold at 11% and 10% on Mallinckrodt and Veridian); with four systems and three kinds of gold the two cannot be separated, and the checks that test the mechanism directly (knowledge-dependence, brief injection) find none. It is reported as the one check that did not come out clean, and it is unresolved at n = 4.
  • Matter rubrics are hand-written and keyword-graded. Each checklist has 16–20 items chosen to span parties, allegations, people, events and outcome; a model can know a fact and phrase it in a way the keywords miss, so recall shares are lower bounds. The 'beyond context' split depends on what the study's task context happens to state.
  • The Jeb Bush evidence prior and metadata probes run on the 600-document local subset (the collection is not redistributable), so their grounding counts are small and their per-request samples are 15 + 15.
  • Lift is estimated on judged documents, whose sampling (TREC's stratified draws for Enron) differs from the collection; a term's lift is relative to the judged set, not to the whole mailbox.
  • The ladder's footprint proxy is rough. Wikipedia pageviews measure public attention, not what is crawlable: two rungs with no article at all are recalled at 80–100% by the mid and large models, and for five matters the lookup falls back to a company or biography page broader than the case (flagged in the table). The rank correlation it supports (≈ +0.5 for the smaller models, ≈ +0.2 for the largest) should be read as "fame is a usable prior for small models", not as a measurement of exposure.
  • The post-cutoff sketches carry their dates. The models decline the 2026 matter partly because the sketch says 2026; a date-free version would test whether they know they do not know. The mid model's confident mis-identification of that matter is a reminder that "named a case" is not "named the right case" — M0 is graded against answer keys, and a wrong confident answer is a hollow mark, not a hit.
  • Ladder rubrics are shorter (10–18 facts) and were written from the public record by the author, not drawn from the study's task contexts; a few items are strict (Equity Funding's reinsurance mechanism, Bhopal's Sevin) and the shares are lower bounds as elsewhere.
  • Veridian's template. The synthetic matter was written to resemble real metal-on-metal hip litigation, and the models say so (§15). Its documents and cast are novel; its archetype is not. That is the right floor for 'knows the documents' and a slightly generous floor for 'knows the kind of case'.
  • Pre-training and distillation are not separable. The code-name swap shows the decision model uses real-world matter knowledge; a base model pre-trained on public text and a model trained on synthetic data generated by a knowledgeable LLM would both show it. No test here distinguishes them.
  • The minimal-edit instrument is noisy. The second-model check confirms the intended flip on only 4–7 of 10 sampled edits per collection, which dilutes label-following toward the old label for every system equally; the within-collection comparison of Jev with the LLMs stands, the contrast with Veridian is the weaker leg.
  • The published-vs-unpublished test has little power on the positive side. Relevant documents in these collections were almost all judged (pool selection), so the unjudged side has a handful of panel-positives and the judged − unjudged difference on them is wide; the negative side is the informative cell, and it shows no judged-set advantage.

24. What this decides

  1. Weight the study's conclusions toward the e-mail corpora and Veridian; report CUAD with the two-channel contamination caveat.
  2. Treat Enron as a known case, not an unknown mailbox. We found no document-level memory (Part I), but the matter is in the model (Part II): the pseudonymised complaint is identified by two of three models (the third names Reliant, a company in the same scandal), the record is recited 85% / 80% / 90%, and naming the company unlocks real, discriminative names. Any Enron result should be reported as "on a matter the model knows".
  3. Report the effect as measured, with its bound — now on three matters. For the LLMs, knowing the case changed review accuracy by at most a few F1 points on Enron, Jeb Bush and CUAD under three manipulations (remove the names, remove the memorised text by paraphrase, inject the case through a brief): no knowledge effect beyond 2.6 F1 points, intervals including zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]); on CUAD, where the documents are memorised, paraphrasing away the memorised surface changed F1 by ≤ 1.3 points and no more on the best-remembered contracts. The knowledge-dependent documents (7% of Enron J) are where help would show, and every system does worse there; supplying the case through a brief on Veridian moved F1 by -0.3 / -0.6 / -1.0 / +0.5 (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev). That still bounds rather than excludes: smaller effects and knowledge that survives every manipulation remain possible, the Enron renaming is a floor because the case is recognised from the fact pattern, and the clean test remains a post-cutoff real matter held out and re-dated as models update. Jev knows the matters, shows no sign of the documents or labels, but its matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] net on Jeb Bush; -18.2 on the FAS 140 request when Enron's vehicles were renamed) — a small familiarity effect of ~2–3 F1 points, consistent with public-web knowledge of public figures and not by itself evidence of training on the collections.
  4. The one anomaly, followed up. Jev lost 18 points of F1 on the FAS 140 request when the Raptor, Talon, LJM2 and Chewco vehicles were renamed, while the LLMs gained. The bare-token check (§21) finds that a header plus one sentence naming a vehicle is called responsive 0% of the time under the request as written (fictional twin 0%) and 78% vs 0% only when the criteria name the vehicles: the loss is not a bare-name reflex but Jev reading request and document together. The code-name swap on four matters then showed the same case knowledge at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs (Enron +17.9, Jeb Bush +34.8, Mallinckrodt +63.2, Endo +10.5 pp). The classifier-native tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections, and they show it knows the cases behind them at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs. Knowing the case did not detectably move its F1 on Enron (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6]).
  5. Pseudonymising the complaint is not a defence. TREC's "Bleak Horizon" was decoded by every model and "Volteron" by 2 of 3 from the fact pattern alone (the other named Reliant Energy, a company in the same scandal), and the nameless ladder sketches identified 26 of 28 real matters for every model. If a client corpus is to be used as a clean test, the documents must be renamed, not just the pleading — and even then the fact pattern remains (§20).
  6. Probe the Anthropic and Gemini models with the same battery (≈ $12 more, dominated by the metadata probe) before publishing cross-vendor comparisons.
  7. Adopt the battery for future corpus selection: any candidate corpus and its matter can be placed on these spectra for a few dollars before a single document is classified. The ladder (§17) is the scale to place it on: at frontier scale, assume any matter with a public docket is known in detail, and treat only post-cutoff matters — or synthetic ones — as genuinely unseen.
  8. Build the next clean real corpus from a post-cutoff matter. The two 2026 SEC actions on the ladder behave like Veridian on recall while being real; a document collection from a matter that arose after the models' cutoff (and is re-dated as models update) would give the study a real-world floor to set beside the synthetic one.
