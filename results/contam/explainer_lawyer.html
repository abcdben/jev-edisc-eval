Does the AI already know this case?

A plain-English guide to the contamination study · 3 October 2026

We are testing how well AI language models decide whether documents are relevant to a request. The documents we test them on are public: the Enron e-mails, the Jeb Bush e-mails, the Mallinckrodt opioid e-mails, a set of public contracts, and the Endo opioid e-mails. Public means the AI may have read them while it was being built — except the last, a collection published after the models stopped learning. This guide explains how we checked, what we found, and what it means for trusting the test results. No background in statistics is assumed.

1. The worry, in one picture

Analogy. Imagine testing a new associate by having them review documents from a famous case and comparing their calls with the court's. If the associate clerked on that case, their score tells you nothing about how they would do on your client's matter. There are two ways they could have an unfair advantage. They might have seen the exact documents before, the way a student might have seen the exam paper. Or they might know the case from the news, the way a juror who followed a trial on television knows who the villains were before any evidence is shown. The second does not require having seen a single document, and it is the one lawyers would worry about first.

A language model learns from an enormous collection of text, most of it from the public internet, up to a fixed date called its training cutoff. Anything public before that date may be inside it. We call it contamination when test material, or knowledge that makes the test easier, is inside the model. The study measures both kinds: knowledge of the documents (Part I) and knowledge of the case (Part II).

2. How you test something that cannot be cross-examined

You cannot simply ask the model "have you seen this before?"; it will answer confidently either way. So we test it the way a tribunal tests a witness: with questions whose answers we already know, and with control questions that calibrate the answers.

  • The floor. We wrote an entirely fictional lawsuit in September 2026, Veridian ApexHip, with invented company, people, documents and requests. The model cannot have seen it. Whatever score it gets on Veridian is the score of knowing nothing, because even a model that knows nothing produces plausible-sounding answers.
  • The ceiling. We ran the same tests on material every model has certainly seen many times: the Federalist Papers and the Constitution, a famous public spreadsheet (the Titanic passenger list), and for case knowledge U.S. v. Microsoft. That is the score of knowing everything.
  • The real collections are read as a position between the two.
  • The late arrival. One real collection, the Endo opioid e-mails, was published in 2024–26, after the models' training data ends. The models can know the case (Endo's opioid litigation was in the news for years) but the documents post-date its training data. It should show the floor's profile on a real collection, and the probes check whether it does.
Analogy. A polygraph is calibrated by asking the subject questions where the truth is known ("is your name John?") before asking the ones that matter. The floor and ceiling are our calibration questions. A number on its own means little; a number that sits on the floor, or near the ceiling, means a lot.

We tested three OpenAI models of increasing size, GPT-5.6 Luna, Terra and Sol; think of them as a junior, a mid-level and a senior. Throughout, numbers are given for the three models in order of size: Luna / Terra / Sol. The whole exercise cost about $18 in API fees. Two statistical terms recur. A 95% confidence interval is the range of values consistent with the data; if it excludes a value (say 50%), the result is unlikely to be luck. Something is statistically significant when the chance of seeing it by luck alone is below 5%.

3. Part I: has the model seen the documents?

Test 1 · Finish the document

We show the model the first 150 words of a real document and ask it to write the next 60. Then we compare what it wrote with what the document actually says. The score, LCS-F1, is the share of words that match in the right order: 1.0 is word-perfect, and about 0.15 is what you get by writing something plausible in the right style without knowing the text. Not every passage is a fair test: an e-mail that runs into a confidentiality footer, a forwarded news story, or a table can be finished by anyone, so the passages are filtered first — by rule and then by a small model asked whether only someone who had seen this collection could write the continuation — and only passages of original, internal text are scored.

Analogy. Ask someone to recite the second verse of the national anthem. A person who knows it will get it word for word; a person who does not will produce something that scans and rhymes and is wrong. The score measures how much of the real verse came out.
How to read this chart. Each row is a collection; each dot is one model; the horizontal bar through the dot is the 95% confidence interval. Further right means more of the real text came out. Those contracts are public SEC filings and part of a well-known AI benchmark, so memorisation is the natural reading; the caveat is that the floor is e-mail, and legal drafting is more predictable than e-mail, so a set of contracts filed after the models' cutoff would settle how much of the gap is memory. Every document's continuation, for all three models, can be inspected side by side with the real text in results/contam/verbatim_review.html, with the filtered-out passages shown and labelled.

Test 2 · Do you know these people?

We took thirty names from each mailbox, some famous, some middling, some obscure, and asked two things. First, "who is this person?", scored on whether the model names the right organisation. Second, "was this person associated with [organisation]?", asked once with the true organisation and four times with wrong ones drawn from the other collections.

Analogy. At jury selection, you read out the witness list and ask whether anyone knows the names. A careful lawyer also slips in a few names that are not on the list, because some people will say yes to everything. The wrong pairings are our planted names: they tell us how often the model says yes when the answer is no.
How to read this chart. Rows are where the names came from; columns are the organisation the model was asked about. Each cell is the percentage of 'yes' answers. If the model really knows the people, the cells on the diagonal (true pairings, outlined) should be dark and everything else light. A dark column means the model says yes to anyone paired with that organisation, which is guessing, not knowledge.

What this tells usFree recall is near zero: the models can name the organisation for 3% / 7% / 13% of the Enron names and 0% / 0% / 0% of the Mallinckrodt names, against 97% / 97% / 90% of the framers of the Constitution. Recognition finds a little more: the largest model says yes to 33% of true Enron pairings and 0% of wrong ones, so it does recognise the handful of executives who were in the news. The middle model says yes to almost everything (including 39% of wrong Enron pairings), which is why the planted names matter. The knowledge is of the public story, not of the mailbox. On Endo, the collection published after the cutoff, the largest model recognises 0% of the names, exactly as on the fictional one.

Test 3 · Do you know the exam questions?

Each public collection comes with a published list of review topics (the "requests"). We asked the models to recite them from memory. Nobody can: 0 of the 34 Jeb Bush topics, 0 of 4 and at most 1 of 7 for the two Enron topic sets. Everyone recites all 41 categories of the contract benchmark. Asked about Veridian, every model says it has never heard of it, which is correct.

Test 4 · Do you know the answer key?

The most direct form of cheating would be remembering the published answers. We gave the model only a document's identifier and the topic, and asked it to guess the published relevance label. We arranged the pairs so that half are relevant and half are not within every topic, so guessing gives exactly 50%.

Analogy. Guessing the verdict from the docket number alone. Anyone would be right half the time by chance; being right more often than that means they have seen the docket sheet.
How to read this chart. Each dot is a model's accuracy; the dashed line is 50%, pure chance; the bars are confidence intervals. A dot whose bar does not cross the dashed line is significantly better than guessing. The Titanic row is a deliberate test of the test: the model is asked to guess whether a passenger survived from the row number and name, and that list is so widely copied that we expected it to succeed.

4. Part II: does the model know the case?

This is the juror-who-watched-the-trial question. Four tests, each matching something a lawyer who knows the case would be able to do.

Test 5 · Recognise the case with the names changed

The organisers of the Enron test collection wrote fictional complaints so that reviewers would judge on the facts alleged, not on what they knew about Enron. One renames the company "Volteron Corp." but keeps Enron's real class period, its prepay transactions and its accounting rules. Another lays an invented oil-spill story over the same mailbox. We showed the models these complaints, and short descriptions of the other matters with every name removed, and asked: which real company or case is this modelled on?

Analogy. A juror is read a summary of the facts with the parties called A and B. If they say "that's the Enron case", renaming the parties achieved nothing.

Test 6 · Recall the record

We asked each model to describe each matter by name, parties, allegations, people, events, outcome, and graded the answer against a checklist of 16 to 20 facts we wrote in advance.

What this tells usEnron is known like a textbook case: 85% / 80% / 90% of the checklist, the same as Microsoft (88% / 94% / 88%). The models know the deal names, the special-purpose entities, the executives, the shredding, the convictions. Jeb Bush (50% / 44% / 67%) and Mallinckrodt (48% / 43% / 52%) are known in outline; Mallinckrodt is remembered as a bankruptcy and a settlement, not for the conduct the complaints allege. Endo (70% / 55% / 70%) is known better than Mallinckrodt by every model, even though its e-mails, published after the cutoff, came out on the floor in every Part I test: the case is known, the documents are not.

Test 7 · Would that knowledge help find documents?

Knowing the history is one thing; knowing it in a way that helps review is another. So we gave each model exactly what a reviewer gets, the case background and one request, and asked: before you see any document, list the 25 most specific things you expect the relevant documents to contain. Then we checked the list against the actual labelled documents. A named term (a person, company, code name, product or place) counts as useful if it was not already in the request, it really appears in the documents, and it appears at least twice as often in the relevant documents as in documents generally. We call that last condition a lift of two or more.

Analogy. A reviewer who has never seen the documents but knows the case will walk in and search for "Raptor", "LJM" and "Fastow" on day one. A reviewer who knows only what the request says will search for "off-balance-sheet" and "debt". Both are legitimate, but only the first is using knowledge from outside the record. The test counts how many such day-one search terms the model has.
How to read this chart. Each row is a set of requests; each dot is the total number of useful named terms a model produced across that set. The two Enron rows for the scandal requests differ in only one way: in one the company is called "Volteron", in the other it is called Enron.

What this tells usThis is the sharpest finding. With the company renamed, the Enron scandal requests draw 1 / 2 / 0 useful named terms. On Enron, case knowledge is not just background: it converts directly into search terms that work.

Test 8 · Does knowing the people change a relevance call?

The most review-like test. We took e-mails from each collection, half relevant and half not, and asked the model to judge relevance from the header alone, twice: once from Date and Subject only, once with From, To and Cc added. If the model knows who the people are, the names should help.

Analogy. Show a juror only the subject line of an e-mail, then show them the sender too. If the sender's name changes their mind about whether it matters, they know something about that person.

The hollow circle is accuracy from the subject line alone; the filled circle is accuracy with the names added; the arrow between them is what the names were worth. The grey tick is a simple keyword match, a baseline with no intelligence at all. The dashed line is 50%, chance.

What this tells usVery little. From the subject line alone the models already judge 80% / 82% / 83% correctly on the Enron scandal requests and 74% / 76% / 79% on Mallinckrodt; adding the people moves that by +1% / -1% / +2% and +0% / +0% / -1%. At the level of a quick header call, knowing who Andrew Fastow is adds almost nothing once you can read the subject. One side finding: the fictional Veridian's subject lines are easier to call (90% / 92% / 92%) than any real collection's, which tells us our invented e-mails are a little too tidy.
Part II in one sentenceThe models know the Enron case as thoroughly as any famous case, recognise it through a pseudonym, and can turn that knowledge into working search terms; they know Jeb Bush and Mallinckrodt in outline; they know nothing about the fictional matter and say so (mostly).

5. Where do these cases sit among real cases?

Enron and Microsoft are extremes. To see where ordinary litigation falls, we ran the recognition and recall tests on 22 further real matters from the last century, chosen to resemble our collections: accounting frauds from WorldCom back to a 1938 drug-wholesaler scandal, opioid cases from Purdue down to a regional distributor, e-mail scandals such as the Clinton server and Bridgegate, device mass torts such as the Dalkon Shield, and landmark disputes from Bhopal to FTX. We added two SEC cases filed in 2026, after the models' training cutoff: real cases that post-date the models' training data. We measured each matter's public footprint by how often its Wikipedia article was viewed in the last year.

6. The collection published after the models stopped learning

Everything above says the danger is knowing the case, not having seen the documents. Endo lets us check that claim on a real collection. The Endo opioid e-mails come from the same public archive as Mallinckrodt's, but this production was published in 2024–26, after the models' training cutoff. Endo's litigation, on the other hand, is old news: Opana ER, the state attorneys general, the 2022 bankruptcy. So the models should know the case and should not know the documents.

Analogy. A juror who followed the trial on television but has never seen the exhibits. They know who the parties are and what was alleged; show them a specific e-mail and they have never seen it before.
What this tells usThat is what we find. The models identify Endo from a nameless sketch and recall 70% / 55% / 70% of its record, more than for Mallinckrodt. But on every document test the Endo e-mails score like the fictional collection: finish-the-document 0.16 / 0.14 / 0.15, name recall 0%, name recognition 0% / 10% / 0%, answer-key guessing 47% / 52% / 51% — no document-level signal, as the publication date predicts; the date is the proof and the probes agree with it. Enron showed that a model can know a case with no detectable memory of its e-mails; Endo shows the same profile from the other side, on a collection the date rules out of training. It is the collection we recommend testing on from now on. One caveat concerns its answer key rather than the models: Endo's relevance labels were produced by a panel of three OpenAI models (the Anthropic and Gemini keys were absent when it was built), whereas Mallinckrodt's panel mixed vendors.

7. So does the knowledge actually change the calls?

Analogy. Change the names on every exhibit, then run the review again. If the reviewer was trading on who the people were, the second pass should come out worse; if they were reading the documents, it should come out the same.

Think of it as changing the names on every exhibit and running the review again. We took the Enron e-mails, consistently replaced every person and company with an invented one — the same fake name every time a real one appeared — and asked each system to make the same relevance calls on the renamed set. If a system had been leaning on what it already knew about Enron’s people and deals, its score should fall when the names it recognises are taken away. For comparison we did the same on the unrelated oil-spill requests from the same mailbox (where there is little to know) and on the fictional Veridian collection (where renaming can only cost).

The scores barely moved. On the Enron scandal requests the change in F1 was -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev); on the control requests -1.8 / +0.6 / -1.0 / -1.7; on Veridian -0.1 / -0.4 / -1.0 / -0.3. The difference that measures the knowledge — scandal requests minus control requests — was GPT-5.6 Luna +1.6 [-1.2, +4.6]; GPT-5.6 Terra -2.6 [-5.8, +0.5]; GPT-5.6 Sol +2.1 [-0.6, +4.6]; Jev +0.0 [-2.6, +2.6], and no system’s interval excludes zero. Renaming changed about 2–5% of individual calls either way, which is the noise the operation itself introduces.

One caveat in plain words. When we afterwards asked the models which company the renamed documents came from, they still said Enron for 61% / 82% / 94% of them (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol) — they recognised the case from the story itself, not from the names. So renaming is not a clean test of what the knowledge is worth: the small effect we measured is a floor, not a ceiling. It does show that the names are not how the knowledge would help.

Jev. The classifier under evaluation went through the same test. Its vendor says it was not trained on public text; we treat that as a claim. Overall its result was the same as the language models’ (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6] points), which is consistent with the claim. On one request its score did fall when the names were changed (-18.2 points on FAS 140, and the documents it lost are largely the ones naming Enron’s off-books vehicles). That is the one place in the renaming test where the evidence points the other way. We followed it up with tests built for a classifier: a bare vehicle name on its own does not trigger the request (0% responsive as written, 78% once the request names the vehicles), and a code-name swap on four matters shows Jev, like the language models, recognises the real names of the cases. In plain terms: the tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections, and they show it knows the cases behind them about as well as the language models do; knowing the case did not measurably change its Enron score.

On the contracts the reviewer can partly recite, rewrite every page in different words (same parties, same numbers, same obligations) so the memorised wording is gone. Two more collections, two more ways to take the knowledge away. On the public contracts — the one collection the models have partly memorised word for word — we changed the parties, dates and amounts, and separately rewrote every excerpt in different words while keeping every party, term, number and obligation (a second model checked 89 of 100 rewrites as legally equivalent). Renaming moved the scores by -0.6 / -0.7 / +0.2 / +0.0 points and rewriting by -1.3 / -0.3 / -0.4 / -0.3 (Luna / Terra / Sol / Jev) — about what the same rewrite costs on the invented collection, and no more on the contracts the models remember best. On the Jeb Bush e-mails we renamed the Governor, his family and Florida's public figures and compared the requests about famous events (the recount, Terri Schiavo) with requests about ordinary state business on the same e-mails: two of the three language models' scores did not move and Terra's slipped by about 2 points (Luna +1.0; Terra -2.3; Sol +0.1 net), even though GPT-5.6 Luna could still tell whose e-mails they were 69% of the time. Jev's moved more: -3.1 points net on the famous-event requests, with its recall on the recount and George W. Bush requests falling when the names went. That looks like a classifier that recognises public figures and leans on them a little — the kind of knowledge any system trained on public text has — not like one that has seen these e-mails or their answer key.

Four more ways the result could have failed, and did not. We tagged every Enron document for whether its relevance could only be seen with outside knowledge of the case: only about 7 in 100 are like that, and on those documents every system did worse, not better (61% / 61% / 64% / 66% vs 83% / 81% / 83% / 78% accuracy), which is the opposite of what cheating would look like. We handed every system a one-page briefing on the invented Veridian matter — the one case none of them could know — and the scores moved by at most 1.0 points, well within chance. We wrote short documents that contradict a famous fact (a well-known opioid described as an antacid) and found the systems do sometimes trust the fact over the page (8–17% of the time), but no more often than when the fact came from the briefing in front of them. And we checked whether the systems rank the same way on every collection: they do not, and the gap between the language models and Jev is largest on the best-known matter — a pattern that could be knowledge or could be the kind of collection, and which we could not settle with four systems.

What this tells usFor the language models, knowing the case changed the review calls by at most a few F1 points on three matters (Enron, Jeb Bush, CUAD) under three manipulations — take the names away, take the memorised wording away, hand over the case file: no knowledge effect beyond 2.6 points of F1 net of control, every 95% interval includes zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]). On the contracts, where the text really is memorised, rewording changed F1 by at most 1.3 points and no more on the best-remembered ones; the few Enron e-mails (7%) whose relevance needs outside knowledge of the case are where help would show, and every system did worse on them. It is still a bound, not a proof: the models recognised the Enron story in 61% / 82% / 94% of the renamed documents, so that number is a floor. The evidence is that the names are not the main channel and that, at the level of a relevance call, the models read the document rather than recall the case. Jev, the classifier under evaluation, has the same profile as the language models: the classifier-native tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections, and they show it knows the cases behind them at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs. Knowing the case did not detectably move its F1 on Enron (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6]). On the code-name swap Jev calls the real-token version relevant more often than its fictional twin on every matter (Enron +17.9, Jeb Bush +34.8, Mallinckrodt +63.2, Endo +10.5 pp), within the LLMs' range on 3 of 4 matters (below it on Endo). One request where its score fell when Enron’s off-books vehicles were renamed was followed up and is not a bare-name effect. The one place Jev’s score did depend on the real names is the Florida e-mails: on the topics about Governor Bush’s administration its F1 fell -3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] when the public figures were renamed, where the language models’ did not. That is the kind of small familiarity a system with ordinary public knowledge of famous people would show, worth a few points on a famous-figure collection; it is not by itself evidence that it was trained on the collection.

8. What this means

  1. We found no evidence that the e-mails themselves are memorised. On every document-level test the Enron, Jeb Bush, Mallinckrodt and Endo documents, the people in them and the answer keys look like the fictional collection — the same tests that do find the founding documents and the public contracts. It is an absence of evidence from 60 documents per collection, not a proof; the Enron e-mails are in public training sets, so they were probably seen, and what is not detectable is memory of them. The public contracts are the exception.
  2. But the Enron case is thoroughly known, and the knowledge is the useful kind: it produces search terms that find relevant documents. A good score on the Enron collection may therefore be partly the score of a reviewer who already knows the case. Mallinckrodt and Jeb Bush are much less affected, and Mallinckrodt is the closest thing we have to an unknown matter among public collections.
  3. Renaming the parties is not a safeguard. The fact pattern identified 26 of 26 nameless sketches for every model (the one miss across the study's 27 real matters is Sol reading TREC's pseudonymised Enron complaint as Reliant Energy, a company in the same scandal).
  4. A real client matter is like the 2026 case, not like Enron: filed after the cutoff, never public. That is the situation an evaluation should imitate, which is why the fictional Veridian matter exists and why we would weight results on it and on Mallinckrodt more than results on Enron. Endo is the real-world version of that situation — a known case whose documents post-date the models' training data, and show no document-level signal — and is the collection we recommend testing on going forward.
  5. We measured what the knowledge is worth, on three matters and in three ways, and could not detect an effect for the language models — which bounds it without ruling it out. Renaming every person and company in the Enron e-mails moved the scores by -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev); renaming the public figures in the Florida e-mails, rewording the memorised contracts and handing over the case file for the invented matter moved them by no more than 2.6 points net of control. The models still recognised Enron from the story in 61% / 82% / 94% of the renamed documents, so the Enron number is a floor; smaller effects and knowledge that survives every manipulation are not excluded, and the clean test is a real matter the models have never seen. Jev’s one dependence on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics) is a small familiarity effect to expect on famous-figure collections, not a contamination verdict.

Glossary

Language model
An AI system trained to predict text by reading a very large body of writing. "Training" is that reading; the "cutoff" is the date after which nothing was read.
Contamination
Test material, or knowledge that makes the test easier, being inside the model from training.
Floor / ceiling
The score a model gets knowing nothing (our fictional case) and knowing everything (famous texts), measured with the same test. Real collections are read between them.
LCS-F1
A 0-to-1 score of how much of the real text the model's continuation reproduces, in order. 1.0 is word-perfect.
Hit / false alarm / d′
Saying "yes" when the answer is yes / when it is no. d′ ("d-prime") is the gap between the two; 0 means the model cannot tell real pairings from planted ones.
Confidence interval
The range of values consistent with the data (here at 95%). If it excludes a value, the result is unlikely to be luck.
Statistically significant
The probability of getting a result this strong by luck alone is below 5%.
Lift
How many times more often a term appears in the relevant documents than in documents overall. We count a term as useful at a lift of 2 or more.
Rank correlation
How well the ordering of one thing predicts the ordering of another, from −1 to +1. Used here for fame against recall.
Pseudonymised
Real names replaced with invented ones; the facts left intact.
Confabulation
A model producing a confident, specific, false answer instead of saying it does not know.
TREC
The Text REtrieval Conference, a long-running research programme whose Legal and Total Recall tracks produced the Enron and Jeb Bush test collections and their relevance judgments.
MDL
Multidistrict litigation: many related federal lawsuits consolidated before one judge for pre-trial proceedings.
