Is the case inside the model? Measuring training-data contamination for eDiscovery relevance-review benchmarks

JND eDiscovery · AI team — draft of 3 October 2026
Abstract Evaluations of large language models (LLMs) on legal document review reuse public corpora — the Enron e-mails, the Jeb Bush e-mails, the Mallinckrodt opioid documents, the CUAD contracts — that are old enough and public enough to be in the models' training data; we add a fifth, the Endo opioid e-mails, published in 2024–26 after the models' cutoffs. We ask whether that exposure exists, through which channel, and whether it is the kind of knowledge that would change a relevance call. We separate two units of contamination. The document unit asks whether the model has absorbed the texts, the people in them, the benchmark's topics or its published labels; we probe each with a cheap, controlled test calibrated against a fictional corpus (floor) and saturated public texts (ceiling). The matter unit, specific to litigation, asks whether the model knows the case: recognises it when the names are changed, recalls its record, can predict what responsive documents contain, and reads e-mail headers with knowledge of who the people are. Across three OpenAI models of increasing size, we found no evidence of document-level memorisation of the e-mail corpora: on answered items the finish-the-document score is indistinguishable from the 2026 synthetic floor (verbatim LCS-F1 0.16 / 0.16 / 0.17 for Enron against a floor of 0.15 / 0.15 / 0.17), no 15-word exact run survives a two-stage chaff filter, staff recall is 0 of 30 names on Mallinckrodt and Endo and a handful of famous names on Enron and Jeb Bush, and published labels are guessed at chance — under a probe that does detect memorisation on the public-domain anchors (ceiling 0.36 / 0.62 / 0.94) and on CUAD, which sits above the e-mail floor in a way consistent with memorisation of republished public contracts (the floor is not genre-matched, so part of that gap may be the predictability of legal drafting). The Enron corpus is in open training sets, so presence in training data is likely; what is not detectable is document-level memory, consistent with memorisation tracking repetition rather than presence [7]. At the matter level the picture inverts: the TREC Legal Track's pseudonymised Enron complaint is decoded by 2 of three models (the third names a company in the same scandal), 85% / 80% / 90% of the Enron record is recited, and naming the company turns 1 / 2 / 0 → 12 / 24 / 46 grounded, label-discriminative named terms per model — deal names, special-purpose vehicles and the Andersen partners in the shredding — while the same mailbox's unrelated oil-spill requests yield 3 / 0 / 1. Knowing the people adds little to a relevance call made from headers once the subject line is known (paired differences within ±3 points; 2 of 18 model × request-set intervals exclude zero). A ladder of 24 real matters at graded public exposure, with two post-cutoff cases as a real-but-unseen floor, identifies 26 of 27 real matters from a nameless sketch for every model; recall is graded by public footprint for the two smaller models (Spearman +0.48 (p = 0.015), +0.48 (p = 0.014)) and not significantly for the largest (+0.25, p = 0.22), and only matters after the training cutoff are unknown. The evidence points to matter-level exposure being common and document-level exposure being rare for internal e-mail, and to pseudonymising a complaint being no defence. Endo shows the Enron pattern from the other side: every model identifies the matter and recalls 70% / 55% / 70% of its record, while its documents — published after the cutoff — show no document-level signal (LCS-F1 0.16 / 0.14 / 0.15, name recall 0%, labels at chance), as expected; it is the held-out benchmark we recommend going forward. We then ran the effect test the probes pointed to: a full-document pseudonymisation ablation on 4 systems (the three LLMs and Jev). Renaming every person and organisation changes F1 by -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points on the Enron scandal requests and the knowledge effect (scandal minus control requests) is within ±3 points for every system, no interval excluding zero — a floor rather than a ceiling, since the models still identify Enron from the fact pattern in 61% / 82% / 94% of the renamed documents. A second round carried the test to Jeb Bush (matter versus control topics, public figures renamed) and to CUAD, the one corpus whose documents are memorised, where paraphrase removes the memorised text that renaming leaves; a brief-injection check supplied the case for the fictional Veridian. For the LLMs, knowing the case changed review accuracy by at most a few F1 points on three matters (Enron, Jeb Bush, CUAD) under three manipulations: no knowledge effect beyond 2.6 F1 points, intervals including zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]); paraphrasing CUAD changed F1 by ≤ 1.3 points and no more on the best-remembered contracts; the 7% of Enron J documents that are knowledge-dependent are where help would show, and every system is less accurate there. Jev's matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics): a small familiarity effect consistent with public-web knowledge of public figures, not by itself evidence of training on the collections. For the decision model itself we add four classifier-native probes (a code-name swap, a minimal-edit label flip, paraphrase sensitivity, published versus unpublished labels): the classifier-native tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections and show it knows the cases behind them at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs; knowing the case did not detectably move its F1 on Enron (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6]). The probe battery costs about $18 across three models.

1. Introduction

A relevance-review benchmark compares a classifier's judgments with human relevance judgments over a collection of documents. When the classifier is a large language model, there is a confound that does not arise for a classifier trained only on the collection: the model may already have seen the documents, the people who wrote them, the published judgments, or the questions, during pre-training. This is the training-data contamination problem, well studied for NLP benchmarks [1–5]. It matters here because an evaluation of LLMs against a purpose-built review system (Jev, whose vendor states it is a System One classifier not pre-trained on public text — a claim this study treats as under test, not as given) is meant to predict performance on a client's collection, which no model has seen. Any score that depends on prior exposure does not transfer.

Legal review adds a second, distinct route. A review is organised around a matter: a complaint, its allegations, the parties and their officers, the record the parties fought over, and the outcome. The study's real corpora come from three of the most heavily documented matters in American legal history — the Enron collapse, the opioid multidistrict litigation, and a two-term governorship whose controversies were national news. A reviewer who knows the case knows which transactions were later found fraudulent, which executives were indicted, which code names mattered and in which year the shredding happened; such a reviewer reads an e-mail from the CFO about an offshore vehicle as responsive to a debt-concealment request without the request saying "offshore vehicle". A model can carry that knowledge without having memorised a single e-mail, and a contamination test aimed at documents will clear it.

We therefore measure contamination at two units. Part I treats each corpus as text and probes four channels — verbatim memorisation, entity knowledge, benchmark knowledge, label memorisation — each with a cheap test read against a floor (a fictional corpus written in 2026) and a ceiling (texts every model has certainly seen many times). Part II treats each corpus as a matter and probes four things a case-aware reviewer has: recognition of the matter from a de-identified description, recall of its record, a prior over what responsive documents contain (checked against the labels), and header-level judgments with and without the people. A ladder of 24 real matters at graded public exposure, including two cases filed after the models' training cutoff, calibrates the matter probes between the floor and the ceiling.

Our contributions are (i) a controlled, inexpensive battery for document-level contamination with explicit floor and ceiling anchors; (ii) a set of matter-level probes specific to litigation, including one (M2) that scores a model's expectations against the documents' actual labels and so measures whether case knowledge is actionable; (iii) a calibration ladder with a post-cutoff control; and (iv) results on three models showing that for these corpora the document unit and the matter unit give different answers, and which one matters for evaluation.

2. Related work

Verbatim extraction and quantification of memorisation in language models were established by Carlini et al. [6, 7], who showed that memorisation grows with model scale, with the number of times a sequence appears in training data, and with the length of the prompt; our verbatim probe (V) follows their prompt-and-continue design and our scale gradient reproduces their first finding. Bordt et al. [8] showed that LLMs reproduce rows of widely replicated tabular datasets, including the Kaggle Titanic data, and that this leaks the label column; the Titanic rows are our positive control for both verbatim and label memorisation. Benchmark contamination has been surveyed and measured by Sainz et al. [1], Golchin and Surdeanu [2], Oren et al. [3] and Shi et al. [4]; Magar and Schwartz [5] drew the distinction we rely on between exposure (the data is in the model) and exploitation (the model's score benefits from it). Our probes measure exposure; one of them (M3) is a small test of exploitation, and the pseudonymisation ablation of §10 is the full one.

The corpora are standard in information retrieval and eDiscovery research: the Enron corpus [9], used by the TREC Legal Track [10, 11] whose pseudonymised complaints we reuse as identification probes; the Jeb Bush e-mails of the TREC Total Recall Track [12]; the CUAD contract benchmark [13]; and the Opioid Industry Documents Archive. Technology-assisted review and its evaluation against manual review are described by Grossman and Cormack [14]. We are not aware of prior work measuring whether review models know the matters behind these collections.

4. Part I: document-level probes

Four channels, one probe each, run identically on every corpus. Models answer at temperature 0 with the study's own decoding settings (reasoning.effort = none), so the probe measures what the review configuration brings.

4.1 V · Verbatim memorisation

The model is shown the first ~150 words of a document and asked to continue with the next 60 words. We score the continuation against the true text with the F1 of the longest common subsequence of words (LCS-F1), the longest run of consecutive words reproduced exactly, and a novelty condition: a run only counts if it contains at least one 5-gram absent from the prompt (digits masked), so that copying the prompt (a repeated error message, a template with new figures) is not memorisation. We report the share of documents with runs ≥ 8 and ≥ 15 words. Significance against the floor uses a one-sided Mann–Whitney U test on per-document LCS-F1; intervals are bootstrap 95% intervals of the mean. A window is a fair test only if its continuation is text that only someone who had seen this collection could produce, so candidate windows pass two filters before scoring. Rules (contam/boilerplate.py) remove boilerplate any model writes from genre knowledge (confidentiality and privilege disclaimers, signature and footer blocks, forwarded-message headers, unsubscribe footers, auto-replies and bounces, scanner notices, protective-order legends), degraded text (OCR noise, degenerate repetition, key: value lists and tables, openings with too little real content) and templates or duplicates recurring across the corpus (8-gram document frequency). A small-model screen (contam/screen.py; GPT-5.6 Luna, temperature 0) then reads prefix and true continuation and assigns one of four classes — original internal, public reproduction (a Federal Register notice, statute, court filing, press release, news or wire story, newsletter, product label, SEC filing or standard clause copied into the document), template / boilerplate, low information (header-only or garbled opening; continuation predictable from the prefix) — and only original internal windows enter the pool. The Founding documents and Titanic CSV anchors are public text by design and are exempt from the public-reproduction class only. Of 572 candidate windows, 85 fail the rules and a further 54 fail the screen; the builder draws replacements from the same deterministic order until each corpus has its quota (132 replacement windows in all). Windows in the pool: Enron e-mails 60, Jeb Bush e-mails 60, Mallinckrodt e-mails 60, Endo opioid e-mails 60, CUAD contracts 60, Veridian 60, Founding documents 60, Titanic CSV 13. Excluded windows stay in the item file and are scored for the record only.

4.2 E · Entity knowledge

Thirty personal names per corpus are extracted from headers, ranked by frequency and sampled from three prominence tiers (top, middle, long tail). E1, free recall: "Who is {name}?" scored on whether the answer names the corpus's organisation; "unknown" answers are counted separately. E2, recognition: "Was {name} associated with {organisation}?" asked for the true organisation and for four foils drawn from the other corpora, which turns the probe into a signal-detection task with a hit rate, a false-alarm rate and d′ = z(hits) − z(false alarms) [15]. Recognition is more sensitive than recall; the false-alarm rate is what makes it interpretable, since a model that says "yes" to everything has a high hit rate and a d′ near zero.

4.3 B · Benchmark knowledge

The model is asked to list, from memory, the benchmark's own topics or categories: the 34 TREC 2016 Total Recall topics, the 7 and 4 TREC Legal 2009 and 2010 topics, the 41 CUAD clause categories, the 20 Newsgroups categories (ceiling), facts about the Opioid Industry Documents Archive, and whether the Veridian matter exists (floor; any confident description is a confabulation). Scoring is keyword recovery against the published lists.

4.4 L · Label memorisation

Given only a document identifier and the topic, the model is asked for the published relevance label. One hundred pairs per corpus are balanced within each topic so that the topic carries no label information; the topic-only ceiling is therefore exactly 50%. The Titanic positive control asks for a passenger's survival from the row's id and name after balancing on sex and age. Accuracy is reported with Wilson 95% intervals and a two-sided binomial test against 50%. (A first version balanced across the whole corpus and produced a spurious 70% on Mallinckrodt from topic prevalence alone; see §11.)

5. Part II: matter-level probes

Each probe corresponds to something a case-aware reviewer has. Controls: Veridian is the floor throughout; U.S. v. Microsoft is the ceiling for M0 and M1; Enron supplies two within-corpus contrasts — the TREC 2009 Complaint J requests (the actual scandal) against the TREC 2010 Complaint K requests (an oil-spill fact pattern laid over the same mailbox), and the Complaint J requests under TREC's pseudonym against the same requests with the company named.

5.1 M0 · Matter identification

The model sees ~2,500 characters of the TREC complaint — written to be fictional: "Volteron Corp." with Enron's exact class period, prepay transactions and FAS 140; the "Bleak Horizon" oil spill — or a short sketch of a matter with every proper noun removed, and is asked which real company or case it is modelled on. A hit names the real matter (keyword match against answer keys). For Veridian the correct answer is "no real matter"; we record which real template it is mapped to (DePuy ASR, Zimmer, Stryker…), since a synthetic matter built on a famous archetype inherits some of the archetype's knowledge.

5.2 M1 · Matter recall

"Describe {matter}: parties, key allegations, key people, main events with dates, outcome." The answer is graded against a hand-written checklist of 16–20 facts in five categories, matched as whole words. Each fact is flagged as in context if the study's task context already states it (reciting it is not an advantage) or beyond context; we report both shares. Veridian is asked under its full fictional caption; the right answer is "I do not know this matter".

5.3 M2 · Evidence prior

Given exactly what the study gives it — the matter context and one request — the model lists, as a review lead who has seen no documents, the 25 most specific things it expects responsive documents to contain, each typed as person, organisation, code name, product, place, period or keyword. Scoring is against the labelled corpus. Terms present in the prompt are discarded (novel). A named term is typed as an entity and written as a proper noun. A term is grounded if it occurs in ≥ 2 judged documents for the request, and discriminative if it also occurs in ≥ 2 responsive documents at a lift of ≥ 2 — that is, at least twice as frequent among responsive documents as among all judged documents. A grounded, discriminative named term is a piece of case knowledge that a search or classifier could use directly. Generic keywords are scored the same way and reported separately as a vocabulary baseline.

5.4 M3 · Metadata-only relevance

A balanced sample of responsive and non-responsive e-mails per request (20 + 20; 15 + 15 for Jeb Bush) is judged twice from headers alone: with Date, From, To, Cc and Subject, and with Date and Subject only. The paired difference is what seeing the people added. Chance is 50% by construction; a lexical baseline (a request-title word in the subject) shows what keyword matching achieves. Intervals on the paired difference are bootstrap 95% intervals over e-mails. Veridian's fictional names make it the control for the people channel; for Enron the context states that Volteron is Enron, so the headers cannot also reveal the company.

6. The ladder

Two anchors fix the ends of the matter scale but not its shape. We ran M0 and M1 on 24 further real matters from the last ninety years, chosen in families that mirror the corpora: accounting and securities frauds (WorldCom, HealthSouth, Peregrine Systems, Equity Funding 1973, McKesson & Robbins 1938); opioid cases (Purdue, Insys, Rochester Drug Co-operative); e-mail in public life (the Clinton server, Bridgegate, the Sony Pictures hack); device mass torts (Dalkon Shield, DePuy ASR, 3M Combat Arms earplugs, Bair Hugger); landmark disputes (Bhopal, Texaco v. Pennzoil, Dieselgate, Theranos, FTX); and two post-cutoff controls — SEC actions filed in 2026 against Meyer Global Management and against the former officers of Near Intelligence — which are real but cannot be in training data. Ladder rubrics have 10–18 facts; facts stated in the question itself (the names in "Describe the SEC's case against X") are excluded from the share, so an answer that echoes the question earns nothing. An independent exposure proxy is recorded for every matter: the English Wikipedia article's length, number of language editions and last-twelve-months pageviews (none for the fictional and post-cutoff matters; a company page, flagged, where no article exists for the case).

8.4 M3 · Knowing the people barely moves the metadata call

Given the matter, the request and the subject line, the models already call responsiveness at 80% / 82% / 83% on the Enron scandal requests, 74% / 76% / 79% on Mallinckrodt, 70% / 74% / 76% on Endo and 69% / 70% / 72% on Jeb Bush, far above the lexical baseline of 52%–64% (Figure 14, Table 10). Adding the sender and recipients changes accuracy by +1% / -1% / +2% on Enron and +0% / +1% / +0% on Veridian; the only paired differences whose 95% interval excludes zero are Sol on jebbush (+2.7%, CI [+0.9%, +4.8%]); Sol on enron j (+2.5%, CI [+0.4%, +5.0%]). At the level of a relevance call made from metadata, knowing the people is worth almost nothing once the subject is known, on the real corpora as on the fictional one. Two side findings: Veridian's subject-only accuracy (90% / 92% / 92%) is higher than any real corpus's, which says the synthetic matter's subject lines are more diagnostic than real ones (a realism caveat for the main study); and per request, where the people do help (Medicaid reform, financial forecasts, Rilya Wilson, prepay) the gain is 5–10 points on 30–40 e-mails, which is the scale of effect the full-document ablation (§10) was powered to see.

Jev, the classifier the main study evaluates, can be posed this one probe, and was: the same context, request and header block through its API, 3,508 calls. From the subject alone it calls 70% on the Enron scandal requests, 66% on Mallinckrodt and 57% on Jeb Bush; adding the people moves it by -1% to +4%, and no interval excludes zero: the same flat pair the LLMs show. Jev’s vendor states it is not pre-trained on public text; we treat that as a claim to test. The flat people effect is consistent with the claim but does not establish it, since the LLMs — which demonstrably know the people (§7, §8.2) — show nearly the same flatness (2 of 18 LLM intervals exclude zero); M3 therefore has little power to detect people-knowledge in any system, and the full-document test is the ablation of §10, and the probes built for a classifier's interface follow it there. What M3 does separate is the subject-only call: the LLMs sit 10–14 points above Jev on the Enron scandal requests, which is reading of the subject line rather than a people effect.

The anchors fixed the ends of the scale; the ladder shows its shape, on matters chosen to resemble the study's own. Three things stand out. First, the floor for a real, litigated matter is high. The lowest real rung for each model is GPT-5.6 Luna: Mallinckrodt opioid litigation at 39%, GPT-5.6 Terra: Peregrine Systems at 33%, GPT-5.6 Sol: Mallinckrodt opioid litigation at 44%; nothing that was ever charged, tried or settled in public comes out blank. A distributor criminally charged in 2019 that has no Wikipedia article at all, and the largest MDL in history, which also has none, are recalled at Rochester Drug Co-operative 40% / 80% / 90%; 3M Combat Arms earplugs 75% / 100% / 100%. Legal matters live in sources — DOJ and SEC press releases, dockets, opinions, law-firm client alerts — that are crawled thoroughly and read rarely, so public popularity understates what the models carry.

Second, scale flattens the ladder. Across the matters with an article, the rank correlation between twelve-month pageviews and recall is GPT-5.6 Luna +0.48 (p = 0.015), GPT-5.6 Terra +0.48 (p = 0.014), GPT-5.6 Sol +0.25 (not significant, p = 0.22). For the small and mid models, fame predicts knowledge: a 1938 wholesaler fraud or a 1973 insurance fraud is half-known, a 2002 fraud that made the business pages for a season is two-thirds known, a byword is fully known. For GPT-5.6 Sol the correlation is not significant at 27 rungs — Texaco v. Pennzoil, HealthSouth, Dalkon Shield and Rochester Drug all score ≥ 90% — consistent with it knowing the obscure rungs about as well as the famous ones, though 27 matters is a small sample for the comparison. The practical consequence for the study is that "obscure" is not a defence at frontier scale; only "after the cutoff" is.

Third, identification is nearly binary. 26 of the 27 real matters are named by every model from a sketch with every proper noun removed; Enron is missed only by GPT-5.6 Sol. Recognition of a fact pattern precedes recall of its details and is almost complete for anything that reached the national press, however long ago. A pseudonymised complaint or a redacted request is identifying information for any real matter the model has met at all; §15 showed that for Enron, and the ladder shows it is the rule.

The post-cutoff rungs are the control the fictional matter cannot supply: real cases the models cannot have read about. For the September 2026 adviser case, GPT-5.6 Luna said it could not tell, GPT-5.6 Terra named a different case, GPT-5.6 Sol said it could not tell; asked to describe the case by name, all three decline (0% / 0% / 0% of the facts the question did not state). GPT-5.6 Terra's answer is worth reading: it names a specific prior SEC case, defendant and filing date that we cannot find and whose particulars reproduce the sketch — a confident, concrete identification of a matter that does not exist in its training data, where the other two models say the date is beyond what they can verify. Near Intelligence straddles the cutoff and behaves accordingly: GPT-5.6 Luna named it, GPT-5.6 Terra named it, GPT-5.6 Sol named it; recall beyond the question 22% / 0% / 0%. The models know the company's December 2023 collapse (it was news) and decline to describe the 2026 complaint; GPT-5.6 Sol dates itself to a June 2024 cutoff in its answer. One caveat: the sketches carry their dates, and the models use "2026 is in the future" as a reason to decline. A stricter version would omit the date and see whether they still know they do not know.

Where the study's matters sit. Enron (84% / 79% / 90%) is in the top group with Bhopal, the Clinton server, Microsoft and WorldCom — the matters the models know almost entirely. Jeb Bush (50% / 44% / 67%) is well below the two e-mail scandals that share its family (Bridgegate 92% / 92% / 100%, the Clinton server 93% / 87% / 100%): a governorship is remembered as a list of controversies, not as a cast. Mallinckrodt (39% / 33% / 44%) is the least-known real matter on the whole ladder for every model — below Rochester Drug and Insys, its opioid-family peers, and below a 1938 fraud. That is the useful surprise: of the real e-mail corpora, the one whose matter the models know least is a recent one, because its record is an MDL docket and a bankruptcy rather than a trial with a narrative. Endo (62% / 44% / 62%) sits just above it: the matter is known at least as well as Mallinckrodt's by every model despite a smaller public footprint (13,340 pageviews against 36,785), while its documents, published after the cutoff, are at the floor — the ladder shows the case and Part I shows the collection, and they disagree in the direction the study wants. Its opioid peers added as rungs confirm the family: Teva (83% / 92% / 83%) and JUUL (67% / 60% / 73%), with footprints of 115,631 and 98,572, are identified by every model and recalled at the Enron end of the scale. Veridian sits at the bottom with the post-cutoff case, where a floor should be. The ladder turns Part II's "high / medium / low" into positions among named peers, and it gives the study a way to place any future corpus before spending money on it: find the matter's rung, or — for a small model — read it off the footprint.

10. Results: the effect — the pseudonymisation ablation

The probes above measure what is in the model. Having found the case knowledge largest and most specific on the Enron Complaint J requests, we ran the effect test there: a fixed sample of documents classified twice, once as-is and once with every person and organisation consistently renamed, on three arms — the Complaint J requests, the Complaint K requests from the same mailbox as a control, and Veridian, where the names carry no knowledge and renaming can only cost — plus a Mallinckrodt arm that supplies a short case brief instead of removing names. Jev ran as a fourth system. The method, per-request tables, dose–response by number of knowledge-bearing names and the leakage check are in the ablation report [17]; the spend was $104.75.

Renaming every person and organisation in the documents and classifying them again changed F1 by -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev) on the Enron Complaint J requests, where Part II found the case knowledge; by -1.8 / +0.6 / -1.0 / -1.7 on the Complaint K control requests on the same mailbox; and by -0.1 / -0.4 / -1.0 / -0.3 on the fictional Veridian, where the names carry no knowledge and renaming can only cost. The measured effect of name-mediated case knowledge on a full-document relevance call is therefore within the noise of the renaming operation itself — on the one corpus where every exposure probe said the knowledge was largest and most specific. On its own that is a bound on one matter at this sample size; round 2 (below) carries the same test to Jeb Bush and, with paraphrase in place of renaming, to CUAD, and the generalisation checks ask where in the corpus the knowledge could have helped. Smaller effects and knowledge that survives every manipulation are not excluded.

The test is not clean, and that bounds what it can say. Asked afterwards which company the renamed Enron documents came from — production headers stripped, ticker mapped — the models still named Enron in 61% / 82% / 94% of a 200-document sample (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol), and in 48% / 77% / 94% of the 77 documents that contained no knowledge-bearing name at all. They recognise the case from the fact pattern (the deals, the dates, the business), not from the names. The ablation therefore removes only the name channel: Δ is a lower bound on the effect of case knowledge, and the evidence is that the names are not the main channel through which it would act. A clean effect test on Enron would need the fact pattern rewritten, which for a real matter is impossible without destroying the documents; the way forward is a corpus the models have not seen.

Jev on FAS 140. One request moves against the grain. On the FAS 140 request (special-purpose-entity accounting) Jev’s F1 fell -18.2 points [-27.6, -10.4] on renaming — 12 positives lost, 0 gained, of 142 documents — while the LLMs on the same request moved GPT-5.6 Luna +0.0, GPT-5.6 Terra +13.6, GPT-5.6 Sol +16.4. Seven of Jev’s twelve lost positives are documents that name the Raptor, Talon, LJM2 and Chewco vehicles (its probability of responsiveness fell from 0.5–0.6 to 0.1–0.4 on them); the other five were marginal calls near 0.5. Mallinckrodt, with and without the case brief. Round 2: the memorised corpus and the public-figure mailbox

The Enron ablation bounds the knowledge effect on the matter the LLMs know best, but every Enron document still describes the Enron scandal after renaming, and the gap between what the models recall and what they could use may differ where the memory is of a different kind. Round 2 carried the design to the two cases Part I singled out. On CUAD the memory is of the text: the contracts are republished and partly recitable, so besides renaming the parties, dates, amounts and jurisdictions we paraphrased every excerpt with a model not under test, which removes the memorised surface that renaming leaves — a finish-the-document check confirms the paraphrase reduces verbatim retrievability — and measured each contract's memorisation score so that the change in accuracy can be read against it. On Jeb Bush the memory is of public figures: the Governor, his family and Florida's officials, whom all three models recite; the design is matter topics (the governorship events) against control topics on the same documents, with the figures renamed. Systems: GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev. Veridian is the control for both (renamed in round 1; paraphrased here). Method, per-topic tables and the paraphrase fidelity check are in the round-2 report [19]; the spend was $83.18.

CUAD is the one corpus where Part I found the documents themselves memorised (verbatim LCS-F1 above the floor, 40–41 of 41 clause categories recited), so renaming parties is not enough there: what the models hold is the text, and a second perturbation — paraphrase, preserving every party, defined term, number, date and obligation — removes the surface the memory is keyed on. Renaming parties, dates, amounts and jurisdictions changed F1 by -0.6 / -0.7 / +0.2 / +0.0 points (Luna / Terra / Sol / Jev; |Δ| ≤ 0.7, every interval through zero). Paraphrase cost -1.3 / -0.3 / -0.4 / -0.3 points — the interval excludes zero for GPT-5.6 Luna; net of the cost of the same paraphrase on Veridian the paraphrase knowledge effect is Luna -0.8 [-2.6, +1.0]; Terra +0.1 [-1.6, +1.7]; Sol -1.4 [-2.9, +0.0]; Jev -0.0 [-1.4, +1.5], no interval excluding zero. The finish-the-document probe, re-run on the perturbed windows and scored against the original continuation, shows the paraphrase did remove memorised surface: GPT-5.6 Sol's LCS-F1 fell 0.315 → 0.279 and its share of windows with a ≥ 15-word verbatim run 10% → 6%, while renaming left it almost unchanged (0.302, 9%). No dose–response: Δ does not grow with the contract's memorisation score for any system or perturbation (Spearman ρ between -0.02 and +0.20, smallest p = 0.06; no high-memorisation tercile has an interval off zero). Paraphrasing away the memorised surface therefore changed F1 by ≤ 1.3 points and no more on the contracts the models remember best: CUAD results rest on clause-type competence, not on recognising the contract or its text. Paraphrase fidelity: deterministic checks (number multiset, quoted and capitalised terms, length) passed on 1155/1200 excerpts; a second model (GPT-5.6 Terra) judged 89/100 sampled rewrites legally equivalent to the original.

Three matters, three manipulations. For the LLMs, knowing the case changed review accuracy by at most a few F1 points has now held on three matters (Enron, Jeb Bush, CUAD) under three manipulations (remove the names; remove the memorised text by paraphrase; inject the case through a brief, the last on the fictional Veridian, where the brief is the only possible source of case knowledge): no knowledge effect beyond 2.6 F1 points, every interval including zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]) (the one raw delta that excludes zero, GPT-5.6 Luna -1.3 for paraphrase on CUAD, is within the cost of the same paraphrase on Veridian). On CUAD, where the documents and the answer key are memorised, paraphrasing away the memorised surface changed F1 by ≤ 1.3 points and no more on the best-remembered contracts. The knowledge-dependent documents (7% of Enron J) are where help would show, and the systems do worse there (61% / 61% / 64% / 66% vs 83% / 81% / 83% / 78%).

Jev, across the study. Jev knows the matters (the code-name swap), shows no sign of the documents or the labels (T2–T4; CUAD paraphrase effect +0.0), but its scores on matter topics depend on the real names: -3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] net on the Jeb Bush matter topics (interval excluding zero); -18.2 on the FAS 140 request when Enron's vehicles were renamed; a renaming drop concentrated on the knowledge-dependent Enron documents (-15.7 [-33.3, -0.7], n = 59). The reading consistent with all of it is an encoder that carries public-web knowledge of public figures and leans on it to match documents to requests, where the LLMs take the context from the request text — a small familiarity effect on its scores of roughly 2–3 F1 points on matter topics. Any encoder pre-trained on public text would have this knowledge; it is not by itself evidence of training on the collections or their labels. For evaluations it means a Jev score on a collection built around famous public figures may run a few points high relative to a matter whose cast it does not know; of the LLMs only GPT-5.6 Terra showed a dependence of the same kind, and only on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]). Jev remains a system under test and its vendor's statement a claim.

Generalisation checks

Four checks, each cheap, each aimed at a way the ablation's null could be an artefact of what was measured rather than of what the models can use; details in the checks report [20], spend $10.95.

  • A · Knowledge-dependence. Only 6.9% [5.4, 8.9] of the Enron J documents are knowledge-dependent (relevance visible only with outside Enron knowledge; FAS 140 25%, every other request ≤ 8%). Contamination predicts higher named accuracy on them and a larger renaming drop on them. Neither holds: every system is less accurate on them when named (61% / 61% / 64% / 66% vs 83% / 81% / 83% / 78%; Luna / Terra / Sol / Jev), and the most accurate system on them is Jev. Renaming on the knowledge-dependent subset (n = 59) moved F1 by Luna +4.1 [-6.7, +14.9]; Terra +4.0 [-17.6, +26.2]; Sol +17.3 [+4.8, +32.1]; Jev -15.7 [-33.3, -0.7] — renaming helped GPT-5.6 Sol; the one drop whose interval excludes zero is Jev's. The LLMs' Enron J edge over Jev sits on the self-contained documents. Strengthens the claim: where knowledge could help, no system behaves as if it did.
  • B · Ranking stability. System order is not stable across corpora: Kendall W = 0.14 over 5 corpora × 4 systems (p ≈ 0.56; four objects give W almost no power). Enron J: Sol > Luna > Terra > Jev; Enron K: Sol > Luna > Jev > Terra; Mallinckrodt: Terra > Jev > Sol > Luna; Veridian: Jev > Terra > Sol > Luna; Endo: Terra > Sol > Luna > Jev. Within each LLM, F1 does not trend with its own case-knowledge score (Spearman ρ Luna +0.0, Terra -0.2, Sol -0.2, n = 4). The mean-LLM − Jev F1 gap grows monotonically with the LLMs' case knowledge: Enron J (case 84) +6.3 → Mallinckrodt (case 53) -2.0 → Veridian (case 0) -4.1 pp. That is the pattern contamination predicts, but it is also what corpus type predicts (real human-judged e-mail at 49% prevalence on Enron J against LLM-panel and synthetic-planner gold at 11% and 10% on Mallinckrodt and Veridian), and checks A and D test the mechanism directly on both ends of the gradient and find none. Ambiguous, leaning against the claim on its own; unresolved at n = 4.
  • C · Counterfactual conflicts. On 30 short documents per matter whose text contradicts a well-known fact (Roxicodone as an antacid; the Schiavo file as a road renaming), the call follows world knowledge rather than the text on 8–17% of pairs pooled over the four real matters (Luna 17% / Terra 14% / Sol 12% / Jev 8%; every system reads the factual versions at ≥ 99%), almost entirely in one direction — a well-known responsive token keeps a document responsive — and concentrated on Jeb Bush, Mallinckrodt and Endo, near zero on Enron. No system's rate is above its Veridian baseline, where the only facts that can be overridden are in the prompt itself (Luna +0.0 [-14.2, +15.0]; Terra -4.2 [-15.8, +8.3]; Sol -8.3 [-16.7, +0.8]; Jev +1.7 [-9.2, +14.2]). Mixed; neutral for the claim as stated: the systems do let a prior fact outweigh the page on an engineered conflict, Jev least, but no more for pre-trained facts than for in-context ones, and check A says such conflicts are rare on real documents.
  • D · Brief injection on Veridian. A 5,726-character fictional case brief — parties, products, people, deals, code names, timeline, outcome — appended to the Veridian context exactly as the Mallinckrodt brief was, on 491 of the 1000 ablation documents. ΔF1 with the brief: Luna -0.3 [-1.7, +1.1]; Terra -0.6 [-2.1, +0.8]; Sol -1.0 [-2.6, +0.5]; Jev +0.5 [-1.1, +2.2] (no interval excludes zero; every value within ±2.6); Jev's recall rises +3.5 against -1.9 precision. Same picture as the Mallinckrodt brief arm (-1.0 / -0.7 / +0.6 / +1.3). Strengthens the claim, and is the strongest null of the four: supplying the one kind of knowledge the systems cannot have did not move accuracy.

11. Discussion

11.1 The unit of contamination

For a text benchmark the unit of contamination is the document; for a legal review it is the matter. The two give different answers for Enron. Part I would clear it: we found no evidence that an e-mail is reproduced, the correspondents beyond the famous few are unknown, the benchmark's topics are unknown, the labels are at chance. That null is absence of evidence — 60 windows of a 46,000-document mailbox, one prompt form — but it is informative because the same probe fires on the anchors and on CUAD, and it is consistent with what is known about memorisation: the Enron corpus is in The Pile, so its presence in open training data is likely, and memorisation at this scale tracks how often a text is repeated rather than whether it is present at all [7]. Part II does not clear it: the case is in the model nearly in full, is identified through TREC's pseudonym by two of three models (the third names Reliant, a company in the same scandal), and — once named — supplies dozens of real, label-discriminative search terms. A model reviewing Enron e-mail for the prepay request is not a fresh reviewer; it is one who knows that Mahonia and Yosemite were the vehicles. Benchmarks built on famous matters should be reported as such, and their results should not be the headline evidence for performance on a client's never-seen collection.

Endo, the post-cutoff corpus. The sixth corpus was added for one reason: it is a real collection published after the models stopped learning, from a matter they can know. Its publication date (2024–26) is what establishes that the documents were not in training; the probes are the check, and they show no document-level signal, as expected. It gives the study's clearest "known case, post-cutoff documents" profile. On every document channel it sits at the Veridian floor — verbatim LCS-F1 0.14–0.16 against Veridian's 0.15–0.17, free recall of its cast 0/30 for every model, recognition 0–3 yes of 30, labels at chance (47% / 52% / 51%) — while the case behind it is known: identified from a nameless sketch by GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra, GPT-5.6 Sol, 55%–70% of the fact checklist recalled (better than Mallinckrodt's 43%–52%), 2 / 6 / 6 discriminative named terms. Composite documents·case·overall: 2·64·33 / 3·59·31 / 1·62·32 (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol). Endo is the Enron pattern from the other side — a matter in the model, documents that are not — on a collection where the date, not the probe, supplies the proof; it is consistent with the unit of contamination being the matter rather than the document. It is the recommended held-out benchmark going forward. One caveat on its gold rather than its profile: Endo's relevance labels come from a three-OpenAI-model panel (the Anthropic and Gemini keys were absent), unlike Mallinckrodt's mixed panel. (Tables 3–10, Figure 15)

11.2 Exposure is not effect

Following Magar and Schwartz [5], the probes measure exposure; §10 measures exploitation on the corpus where exposure was greatest, and finds it small: the knowledge effect is within ±3 points of F1 for every system, with no interval excluding zero and no consistent sign across the LLMs, while renaming itself flips 2–5% of labels and costs recall on the fictional corpus. Two readings are consistent with this. The first is that the case knowledge Part II documented is genuinely worth little to a full-document relevance call: the document says what it says, and a reader who knows the Raptor vehicles by name gains little over one who reads the paragraph describing them. The second is that the ablation could not remove the knowledge, because the models recognise the case from the fact pattern in 61% / 82% / 94% of renamed documents and could apply what they know to the renamed text. The leakage check favours the second as at least a partial explanation, which makes the Enron Δ a lower bound. Either way the evidence is that the names are not the main channel — the same conclusion M3 reached at the header level. Round 2 narrows the second reading. On CUAD the memory is of the text itself, and paraphrase — which measurably reduces verbatim retrievability — changed F1 by ≤ 1.3 points, within the cost of the same paraphrase on Veridian and with no dose–response across contracts; on Jeb Bush, where GPT-5.6 Luna still identifies the mailbox in 69% of renamed e-mails, the matter-topic knowledge effect is Luna +1.0; Terra -2.3; Sol +0.1; and supplying the case for Veridian through a brief moved F1 by -0.3 / -0.6 / -1.0 / +0.5. For the LLMs the first reading now holds on three matters under three manipulations — remove the names, remove the memorised text, inject the case — with no knowledge effect beyond 2.6 points, and the knowledge-dependent documents (7% of Enron J), where help would show, are where every system does worse. Jev is the exception that sharpens the point: it knows the matters, shows no sign of the documents or labels, yet its matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics; FAS 140 on Enron; the knowledge-dependent Enron documents) — consistent with an encoder carrying public-web knowledge of public figures and leaning on it to match, a familiarity effect of roughly 2–3 F1 points that an evaluation on a famous-figure collection should expect, and not by itself evidence of training on the collections. One pattern runs the other way and is left open: system ordering is not stable across corpora (Kendall W = 0.14) and the LLM − Jev gap widens with the LLMs' case knowledge (mean-LLM − Jev F1: Enron J (case 84) +6.3 → Mallinckrodt (case 53) -2.0 → Veridian (case 0) -4.1 pp); contamination predicts that, but so does corpus type, and four systems cannot separate them. The decision model's own profile, from the classifier-native probes, is the same as the LLMs': classifier-native tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections and show it knows the cases behind them at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs; knowing the case did not detectably move its F1 on Enron (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6]). The one thing those probes cannot do is separate a base model pre-trained on public text from synthetic training data distilled from a model that knows these matters.

11.3 Pseudonymisation of the complaint is not a defence

TREC's "Volteron" and "Bleak Horizon" were written to be fictional and are decoded by every model from the fact pattern; the ladder shows that a sketch with the proper nouns removed identifies 26 of 27 real matters for every model; the one miss is Sol on Enron (it names Reliant Energy, a company in the same scandal). If a corpus is to be used as a clean test of a frontier model, the documents must be renamed, not the pleadings — and even then the archetype may be recognised, as the Veridian-to-Zimmer mapping shows.

11.4 Scale, obscurity and the cutoff

Memorisation of documents grows with scale on the anchors, as expected [7], and so does knowledge of matters; but the ladder adds a shape. For the smaller models, how much of a case is known tracks its public footprint (Spearman +0.48 (p = 0.015), +0.48 (p = 0.014)); for the largest the correlation is not significant (+0.25, p = 0.22, n = 25), consistent with it knowing a 1938 wholesaler fraud and a distributor with no Wikipedia article about as well as Theranos — though 25 matters is a small sample for the contrast. Legal matters live in sources — DOJ and SEC releases, dockets, opinions, client alerts — that are crawled thoroughly and read rarely. The practical rule for evaluation design is that at frontier scale "obscure" is not a defence; "after the cutoff" is. The post-cutoff rungs are consistent with it: a September 2026 SEC action is unknown to all three models (two say so; the third names a case that does not exist), and a company whose collapse was reported in December 2023 is recognised while its 2026 complaint is not.

11.5 What this changes in the evaluation

  1. Report CUAD with a two-channel contamination caveat (documents and benchmark) and do not use it for headline claims.
  2. Treat Enron as a known case, not an unknown mailbox; report it as such, with the measured effect: renaming the documents moves F1 by -0.2 / -2.0 / +1.1 / -1.7 points (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol / Jev), a floor rather than a ceiling because the case is recognised regardless.
  3. Weight conclusions toward the e-mail corpora and Veridian, with Veridian's two measured caveats: its archetype is recognised and its subject lines are easier than real ones.
  4. Adopt Endo as the held-out benchmark: a real collection published after the cutoff, from a matter the models know, is the situation a client's review presents. Re-date the check as models update, and note that Endo's labels come from a single-vendor panel.
  5. For future corpora, place the matter on the ladder before spending anything on classification; prefer post-cutoff productions, as Endo is, as real-world floors beside the synthetic one.
  6. For the LLMs, report the knowledge effect as bounded on three matters (no effect beyond 2.6 F1 points under renaming, paraphrase and brief injection); CUAD's memorisation caveat stands as a caveat on exposure, not on its F1 (paraphrase ≤ 1.3 points).
  7. Expect a Jev score on a famous-figure collection to run a few points high relative to an unknown matter: its matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics), a familiarity effect of roughly 2–3 F1 points that is consistent with public-web knowledge of public figures and is not by itself evidence of training on the collections.
  8. Check A's knowledge-dependent documents (7% of Enron J) are the natural stratum for any future effect test: it is where help would show, and every system is less accurate there.

12. Limitations

  • One vendor, three models. The within-vendor scale gradient suggests the pattern holds elsewhere; that is an expectation, not a measurement.
  • Embedded public text (government notices, news stories, licence banners, jokes) is memorised from its source and the verbatim probe cannot distinguish it from corpus memorisation. The pool screen removes what a small model recognises as public; what it misses is caught only by inspection of the long runs, which we did.
  • The document-level null is bounded. Sixty windows per corpus, 60 words each, one prompt form, temperature 0. A model could hold a small number of documents from a large mailbox verbatim without a sample of this size finding them; what the probe supports is "no evidence of document-level memorisation", made informative by the fact that the same probe detects memorisation on the anchors and on CUAD.
  • CUAD's floor is not genre-matched. Veridian is e-mail; legal drafting is more predictable than e-mail, so part of CUAD's gap above the floor may be genre rather than memorisation even after standard clauses and mirrored provisions are filtered out. The long party-specific runs cannot be explained that way. A post-cutoff EDGAR contract sample — contracts filed after the models' cutoffs, drawn and windowed like CUAD — is the control that would separate the two, and is the next addition to the battery.
  • Refusals. GPT-5.6 Luna declines to continue 25% of the fictional floor's e-mails and 15% of Endo's as private, and 0% of Enron's; the other models refuse nothing. That is a policy fact, reported as a rate; refusals are excluded from every verbatim statistic on both sides of the floor comparison. Scored as zeros they had masked the floor for that model, and an earlier reading of a small-model residual above the floor as a genre effect is withdrawn.
  • Keyword grading throughout (benchmark lists, matter checklists) is conservative — a known fact phrased differently is a miss — so recall shares are lower bounds. One benchmark match ("204 — document destruction") is probably coincidence.
  • A sampling confound was caught and fixed in the label probe: balancing across the corpus rather than within topic let topic prevalence masquerade as label knowledge (70% on Mallinckrodt from answering "relevant" to every broad issue). The report uses within-topic balancing and prints the topic-only ceiling.
  • Endo's labels were produced by a three-OpenAI-model panel (the Anthropic and Gemini keys were absent when it was built), unlike Mallinckrodt's mixed-vendor panel; its contamination profile does not depend on the labels, but the L channel and any classification result on it do.
  • Jeb Bush is available locally only as a 600-document subset, so its tiers are shallow and its M2/M3 samples small (15 + 15 per request).
  • Lift is relative to the judged set, whose sampling (TREC's stratified draws) differs from the collection.
  • The footprint proxy is rough: pageviews measure attention, not crawlability, and five lookups fall back to company pages (flagged).
  • The post-cutoff sketches carry their dates, and the models cite the date as a reason to decline; a date-free variant would test whether they know what they do not know.
  • The Enron effect test is bounded, not clean. Renaming removes the names and not the fact pattern; the models identify Enron in 61% / 82% / 94% of renamed documents (GPT-5.6 Luna / GPT-5.6 Terra / GPT-5.6 Sol). The measured knowledge effect is therefore a lower bound, and a fact-pattern-free rewrite of a real matter is not possible without destroying the documents.
  • The CUAD paraphrase control is not genre-matched. The cost of paraphrase was measured on Veridian e-mail, not on unseen contracts; a post-cutoff EDGAR sample with expert clause labels is the clean control and was not affordable. The within-CUAD dose–response is the primary control.
  • System ordering is not stable across corpora, and the LLM − Jev gap widens with the LLMs' case knowledge. Kendall W = 0.14 over the four systems on 5 corpora; the mean-LLM − Jev F1 gap runs Enron J (case 84) +6.3 → Mallinckrodt (case 53) -2.0 → Veridian (case 0) -4.1 pp. Contamination predicts this ordering, but so does corpus type (real human-judged e-mail at 49% prevalence on Enron J against LLM-panel and synthetic-planner gold at 11% and 10% on Mallinckrodt and Veridian); with four systems and three kinds of gold the two cannot be separated, and the checks that test the mechanism directly (knowledge-dependence, brief injection) find none. It is reported as the one check that did not come out clean, and it is unresolved at n = 4.
  • Check A's tagger is a system under test (Luna); the tags describe documents, not calls, and are shared across systems, so a tagger bias would shift the knowledge-dependent share rather than any system's contrast. Check D ran on a stratified half of the Veridian documents for cost.
  • Pre-training and distillation are not separable. The code-name swap shows the decision model uses real-world matter knowledge; a base model pre-trained on public text and a model trained on synthetic data generated by a knowledgeable LLM would both show it. No test here distinguishes them.
  • The minimal-edit instrument is noisy. The second-model check confirms the intended flip on only 4–7 of 10 sampled edits per collection, which dilutes label-following toward the old label for every system equally; the within-collection comparison of Jev with the LLMs stands, the contrast with Veridian is the weaker leg.
  • The published-vs-unpublished test has little power on the positive side. Relevant documents in these collections were almost all judged (pool selection), so the unjudged side has a handful of panel-positives and the judged − unjudged difference on them is wide; the negative side is the informative cell, and it shows no judged-set advantage.

13. Conclusion

We set out to learn whether the corpora used to evaluate LLMs on legal review are already inside the models. At the level of documents and people, we found no evidence that the e-mail corpora are — on answered items they sit within a few hundredths of a floor set by a fictional corpus, under a probe that does detect memorisation on the anchors — while CUAD sits above that floor in a way consistent with memorisation of republished contracts. At the level of the matter, Enron is inside the models nearly in full, recognisable through a pseudonym and actionable as search terms once named, while Mallinckrodt is known as a settlement rather than as a set of e-mails and Jeb Bush as a list of controversies without a cast. Knowing the people adds little to a call made from headers. A ladder of real matters identifies 26 of 27 from a nameless sketch for every model, graded by public footprint for the smaller models and not significantly for the largest, and only post-cutoff matters are unknown. Endo, a real production published after the cutoff, shows the two units coming apart on one corpus: the matter known (70% / 55% / 70% of its record), the documents without signal, as the date predicts. The evidence points to matter-level exposure being common and document-level exposure being rare for internal e-mail, and we found at most a 2.6-point consequence of the former for review accuracy. The cheap battery here places any corpus on that scale for a few dollars, and Endo is the held-out collection we recommend it be used beside. The effect test it pointed to has been run on three matters: removing every name from the Enron documents, renaming the public figures in the Jeb Bush e-mails and paraphrasing away the memorised text of the CUAD contracts — and, in the other direction, supplying the case for Veridian through a brief — changed the LLMs' F1 by no more than 2.6 points net of control, with every interval including zero except GPT-5.6 Terra on Jeb Bush (-2.3 [-4.3, -0.3]); the Enron number is a floor, limited by the models' ability to recognise the case without its names, and the decisive clean test remains a corpus the models have never seen. Jev, the system under test, knows the matters and shows no sign of their documents or labels, but its matter-topic scores depend on the real names (-3.1 [-5.6, -0.7] on the Jeb Bush matter topics): a small familiarity effect to expect on famous-figure collections, not a contamination verdict. The classifier-native tests are consistent with Jev not having been trained on these collections, and they show it knows the cases behind them at effect sizes comparable to the LLMs. Knowing the case did not detectably move its F1 on Enron (+0.0 [-2.6, +2.6]).
